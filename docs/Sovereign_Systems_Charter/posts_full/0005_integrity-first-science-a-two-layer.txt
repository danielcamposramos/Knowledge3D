Integrity-First Science: A Two-Layer Framework harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Integrity-First Science: A Two-Layer Framework A governance blueprint for any high-stakes scientific domain. harry blazer Feb 23, 2026 Share In high-stakes domains where scientific conclusions influence markets, policy and human freedom, the integrity of the incentive structure matters as much as the quality of the data. Complex systems science operates under uncertainty, probabilistic inference and evolving models. When institutional pressures, funding asymmetries, reputational risk or policy entanglement distort those dynamics, trust erodes. What follows is a structural proposal: a two-layer framework designed to make honesty the path of least resistance, to elevate refutation alongside confirmation and to preserve uncertainty without collapsing into paralysis. If science is to remain a truth-seeking enterprise in an age of enormous stakes, its incentive architecture must be intentionally designed. (Constucted with the help of CHAT) LAYER I: CONSTITUTIONAL PRINCIPLES (The Non-Negotiables) 1. Truth-Seeking Over Narrative Scientific institutions exist to approximate truth, not to defend narratives, markets or policy agendas. 2. Refutation Is Co-Equal With Confirmation Any system that rewards confirmation more than disconfirmation will drift toward distortion. Refutation must carry equal prestige, funding, and career viability. 3. Transparency Is the Default Opacity requires justification. Data, methods, assumptions and funding must be visible by default. 4. Uncertainty Must Be Preserved Uncertainty is not weakness. It is information. Scientific communication must distinguish: Measurement Interpretation Projection Policy preference 5. Measurement and Advocacy Must Be Separated Scientific bodies may measure and analyze. They must not launder values as “science.” Policy recommendations must be clearly distinguished from evidentiary findings. 6. No Monopoly on Legitimacy No single institution, model family, journal or funding body may claim epistemic finality. Plurality of models and measurement systems is a safeguard, not a flaw. 7. Prediction Over Persuasion High-stakes claims must generate testable forecasts. Accuracy over time is the ultimate credibility filter. 8. Integrity Is Always Possible Incentives may shape behavior. They do not eliminate moral agency. Systems must lower the cost of integrity and raise the cost of distortion. LAYER II: STRUCTURAL IMPLEMENTATION (The Enforcement Architecture) 1. Funding Architecture A. Mandatory Allocation Split All major research programs must allocate fixed minimum percentages to: Discovery (new hypotheses) Validation (replication and measurement) Adversarial testing (structured refutation) No discretionary bypass permitted. B. Opposing Grant Structure For policy-relevant hypotheses: Equal funding for hypothesis H Equal funding for strongest alternative ¬H Shared datasets and protocols Guaranteed publication rights This prevents epistemic capture through resource imbalance. C. Refutation Incentives Create formal rewards for: Demonstrating model fragility Identifying misestimated parameters Publishing failed replications Exposing confounds Career prestige must attach to epistemic correction. 2. Publication & Review Reform A. Registered Reports Default Design reviewed before results exist. Acceptance based on methodology, not conclusion. B. Open Technical Stack Policy-grade research requires: Full code publication Data pipelines Parameter settings Compute environments Negative results archive C. Two-Lane Review All high-impact work receives: Standard peer review Independent compensated red-team review 3. Reputation & Career Metrics Replace citation dominance with: Replication success rate Forecast calibration record Correction transparency Robustness across datasets Replication and auditing become viable career tracks. 4. Model Governance A. Model Cards Required All major models must publish: Known limitations Sensitivity maps Failure domains Uncertainty bounds Conditions of misuse B. Competing Model Commons Require at least three independent model architectures using identical benchmark data. Public performance scoreboards maintained. C. Forecast Scoring All major projections timestamped and later scored. Calibration metrics publicly tracked. 5. Data Integrity Safeguards A. Redundant Independent Measurement Key variables measured by: Multiple nations Multiple instruments Multiple institutions Cross-calibrated regularly. B. Immutable Provenance Ledger All datasets must maintain: Version history Processing changes Rationale logs Audit trail No silent adjustments. 6. Science–Policy Separation A. Two-Document Rule Every policy-relevant output must publish: Science Report (findings, uncertainty, limits) Policy Memo (values, tradeoffs, recommendations) No blending. B. Policy Stress Testing Policies must be evaluated against: Low-impact scenario High-impact scenario Alternative causal frameworks Natural variability dominant models Adversarial governance required. 7. Institutional Safeguard: Scientific Adversarial Commons (SAC) An independent body tasked solely with: Replication oversight Model stress testing Data audits Forecast scoring Whistleblower protection Charter constraint: SAC cannot advocate policy. It grades evidentiary strength only. The System Test A system is integrity-first if: Being wrong is survivable. Correcting error increases prestige. Dissent is funded, not punished. Data changes are traceable. Models are publicly stress-tested. Policy cannot claim more certainty than evidence supports. If those conditions hold, integrity becomes low-friction. © Harry Blazer.
