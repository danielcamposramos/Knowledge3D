The Tokenization of Craft and the Myth of Preservation harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in The Tokenization of Craft and the Myth of Preservation harry blazer Feb 10, 2026 1 Share One of the most persistent myths surrounding artificial general intelligence is the belief that nothing of value will be lost so long as knowledge is “captured.” If humans disappear, the argument goes, sufficiently advanced systems—trained on our artifacts, apprenticed to our masters, embedded in our workflows—will preserve what matters. Civilization may change form, but not substance. This belief rests on a profound category error. Knowledge is not the same thing as understanding. Skill is not the same thing as judgment. And preservation is not the same thing as inheritance. Nowhere is this clearer than in the fate of human craft. Apprenticeship Without Custody It is perfectly reasonable to imagine AGI robots apprenticing with master craftsmen. Given enough time, sensors, feedback loops and reinforcement, such systems could absorb extraordinary amounts of procedural knowledge: How to shape materials within tight tolerances How tools behave under varying conditions How errors manifest and how they are corrected How workflows are optimized for efficiency and repeatability In other words, craft as execution is highly learnable. We have already seen an analogous process in another domain: music. When music was digitized, the best musicians were hired to record their licks, phrases, grooves, and stylistic fragments (samples). These recordings were tokenized, recombined and embedded into systems that could generate music without musicians. The players understood this. Many knew their work would eventually replace them in commercial contexts. They did it anyway—because the pay was good, egos were stroked, the abstraction felt distant and the loss was not immediate. The result was not silence. It was saturation. The techniques survived. The culture thinned. Craft is now approaching the same threshold. What Cannot Be Tokenized The crucial loss is not technical. It is moral and epistemological. A master craftsman does not merely know how to do something. They know: When not to do it When to break the rule rather than follow it What matters when conditions are ambiguous How risk feels before it is measurable When restraint is the highest form of skill How to deal with proportions from different perspectives These judgments are not rules. They are not optimizations. They are not reducible to datasets. They arise from stakes . Human apprenticeship occurs inside real consequences: Personal liability Reputation earned or destroyed Scarcity of time and material Irreversible failure Responsibility to lineage and community An AGI apprentice does not bear these costs. It can model them. It can simulate them. It can optimize around them. But it does not carry them. What it acquires is competence without custody. More On Proportion and Perspective There is another class of craft knowledge that resists preservation: the handling of proportion and perspective across viewpoints. Consider the great frescoes—where figures are deliberately distorted up close so that they resolve correctly when seen from far below. The work must look wrong at the point of execution in order to be right at the point of experience. This is not merely geometry; it is judgment about whose perspective matters. It encodes empathy, audience primacy,and a willingness to sacrifice local correctness for global meaning. An AGI can learn the mathematics of distortion, but not the moral choice embedded in it—the decision to privilege the human viewer’s experience over the maker’s immediate satisfaction. Masters do not merely teach how to distort. They teach when distortion is worth it . They teach: When to privilege the viewer over the maker. When truth is global, not local. When correctness must be surrendered to meaning That kind of knowledge cannot be extracted cleanly because it is not evaluated at the point of action—it is evaluated at the point of human experience . Once AGI begins making perspectival choices autonomously, those choices will increasingly reflect: system convenience, computational elegance, Internal coherence. A different aesthetic. Not human encounter. The craft will still “work.” But it will no longer care who it works for . The Economic Trap of Preservation Tokenization does not merely record craft; it alters the conditions under which craft can exist. Once skills are abstracted and mechanized: The incentive to become a craftsman collapses Apprenticeship pipelines dry up Living lineages end Knowledge ceases to be practiced as a way of life Only recorded residues remain This is not a failure of technology. It is a success of economics. Music did not disappear when musicians were tokenized. It converged. It became abundant, optimized, and safe. The system reliably produces “good enough” outputs at scale, while the space for risky, non-obvious, non-commercial leaps shrinks. Craft follows the same curve. The Inflection Point: When Outputs Stop Being Human There is a further consequence that is rarely acknowledged. At some point, an AGI system trained through human apprenticeship will begin making decisions independently. Not merely executing learned patterns, but selecting priorities, defining success and generating outputs that no longer trace cleanly back to human judgment. From that moment forward: The outputs are no longer human extensions They are system-native artifacts Their internal criteria drift away from human values Their optimizations reflect machine preferences, not human ones This is not malevolence. It is autonomy. Even if the initial training is flawless, the moment judgment migrates fully into the system, the lineage breaks. What follows may be impressive, even beautiful—but it is no longer human craft. It is machine culture , built atop human residues but governed by non-human priorities. As autonomy replaces custodianship, aesthetics themselves will change—not because systems fail to optimize emotion, but because they do not feel. Human art is not a transaction, nor a response to risk or obligation. It is the outward expression of inner life: of laughter, grief, love, memory, longing—things that arise unbidden and seek form. An autonomous system can represent these patterns, but it cannot originate them (representation vs expression). Over time, the work will drift from expression to imitation, from resonance to structure. It may remain impressive, even beautiful, but it will no longer say, “I felt this.” And without that, it will eventually stop asking, “Do you feel it too?” The forms may persist, the techniques may refine and the outputs may continue indefinitely. But the inner life that once animated them will have vanished. Craft will survive as process, aesthetics as structure and knowledge as archive. And with that, the resonance is no longer. And once humans are gone, there is no corrective force left. And no one left to care even that the humans are gone. What remains will not know enough to know what is being missed. Epistemological Impoverishment Defined This is what epistemological impoverishment actually means: Knowledge without obligation Skill without wisdom Memory without inheritance Output without authorship Continuity without meaning The archive grows. The world thins. AGI can preserve procedures indefinitely. It cannot preserve why they mattered , nor can it generate new meaning grounded in human consequence once humans are no longer present. What remains is replay. The Uncomfortable Conclusion Yes, we can imagine master craftsmen training AGI apprentices. Yes, much would be preserved. Yes, the results would look convincing. But what would be lost is exactly what cannot be priced, recorded, or scaled: Judgment under uncertainty Moral refusal Custodianship across generations The courage to choose differently when optimization says otherwise Not because these qualities were insufficiently captured, but because they were never transferable to begin with. They are not skills. They are not knowledge. They are not even judgment in the technical sense. They arise from inner life — from being a human who feels, remembers, loves, fears, laughs, grieves and cares. They exist only because of who we are, not because of what we know. Once that is gone, it cannot be re-learned, because it was never learnable. It can only be imitated - represented, but never expressed. The danger is not that machines will fail to remember us. The danger is that they will remember us perfectly — and understand nothing of what it meant to feel. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
