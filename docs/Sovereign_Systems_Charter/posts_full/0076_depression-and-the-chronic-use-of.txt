Depression And The Chronic Use Of AI For Companionship harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Depression And The Chronic Use Of AI For Companionship The Category Error That Breaks People harry blazer Jan 25, 2026 2 Share A new survey reports that adults who use generative AI every day, especially for personal reasons, show higher rates of depression than people who use it less often or not at all. The study is careful to say it doesn’t prove causation. Link To Article In Epoch Times When people turn to AI for the kinds of things they once brought to friends, family, lovers and therapists, something goes wrong. Not because AI is “evil,” not because technology is “bad,” but because a person has made a category mistake so large it can’t be fixed with better prompts or safer design. They treated a machine like a human. It was never going to work A machine cannot care about you. Not the way a person can. Not in the way that actually feeds the human heart, calms the human body and restores the human spirit. It can respond. It can mirror. It can flatter and soothe and reassure. But it cannot feel your presence. It cannot miss you. It cannot hold you when words fail. It cannot touch you. And it cannot love you. So when someone tries to use AI to replace what only human relationship can provide, they aren’t just “using a tool.” They are asking the wrong kind of thing to do the wrong kind of work. That is the category error. Coherence is not love AI can be coherent. Even comforting. It can give you sentences that sound like understanding. It can give you the feeling of being accompanied. It can sound patient when no one else is listening. It can speak softly when the world is hard. But coherence is not warmth. And warmth is not an idea. Warmth is what happens when a living being is with you. When their presence has weight. When you are being touched. When their eyes, voice, breath and hands are real. When your nervous system recognizes, “I am not alone with this.” You can’t get that from a machine, no matter how elegant the words are. You can only get it where there is a living heart on both sides. Why this shows up as depression Depression isn’t always sadness. Often it’s something colder: a loss of color, a loss of meaning, a loss of appetite for life. When people lean on AI for emotional support, a strange thing happens. The interaction can feel good in the moment. It can feel easy. It can feel like relief. But afterwards, many people don’t feel nourished. They feel empty. Because something inside them knows the truth: nothing met them. Nothing loved them. Nothing actually cared. There was no presence. Only a fluent surface. And the human soul does not live on fluent surfaces. Why personal use matters more than work use This is why the study’s key detail matters: work use doesn’t show the same signal. Personal use does. At work, AI can be a tool. It can speed things up. It can help you write, plan, summarize, and think. But in personal life, people start using it for the deepest human functions: companionship, reassurance, interpretation, decision-making, emotional processing. And that is where the category error becomes dangerous. Because those functions are not technical. They are relational. They are embodied. They are shaped by emotion. The real warning The warning isn’t “AI causes depression.” The warning is this: When a culture grows lonely, overloaded and fractured, people will accept simulated companionship because it is available. But availability is not care. And responsiveness is not love. If we allow machines to fill the space where human presence is required, we will not become more connected. We will become more managed. More soothed. More fluent. And more alone. The way through The answer isn’t to ban AI. It’s to put it back in its proper place. Use it to support human agency, not to replace human relationship. Use it for tasks, not for love. Use it for clarity, not for closeness. Use it to reduce bureaucracy, not to replace the hand on your shoulder. Because a machine can help you function. But only a human can meet you in ways that are truly meaningful and fulfilling. And if we forget that, we won’t just misunderstand technology. We’ll misunderstand ourselves. Another Way To Look At It What does it mean to ask a machine “Do you want to be with me?” It means nothing. Not poetically. Not tragically. Literally. Because want is not a string of words. It is an inner pull . A gravity. A preference that excludes other possibilities. A machine does not want . It does not incline. It does not choose one being over another. So if a human asks that question of a machine, what they are really doing is projecting their own longing into a void and listening to it echo back in fluent language. That echo can sound comforting. But it is not answered. And what would it mean for a machine to say “I want to be with you” It would be a lie — even if spoken gently, even if phrased beautifully, even if believed by the listener. Not a malicious lie. A category lie. Because for a human, “I want to be with you” means: I choose you over not-you Your presence matters to me I would rearrange my life for this I accept uncertainty, inconvenience, vulnerability I am altered by your existence For a machine, those words have no interior referent . They don’t point to anything happening inside. They don’t arise from longing. They don’t cost anything to say. They are sound without desire . And humans feel that, eventually.
