What LLM (Large Language Models) like CHAT Are Good For and What They&#x27;re Lousy At harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in What LLM (Large Language Models) like CHAT Are Good For and What They're Lousy At A Confession regarding the Epistemic Limits of Large Language Models harry blazer Feb 09, 2026 Share One of the most confusing experiences for users of modern AI systems is this: How can the same system be astonishingly insightful one moment and confidently wrong the next? This isn’t a minor annoyance. It’s a structural issue — and misunderstanding it leads people to either overtrust AI or dismiss it entirely. Both are mistakes. The truth is subtler, and more important. The Myth of Universal Intelligence Large Language Models (LLMs) like ChatGPT are often spoken about as if they are general problem-solvers with roughly human-like access to knowledge. That mental model is wrong. An LLM is not: a continuously browsing agent a technician with hands-on experience a database with guaranteed current information an oracle with privileged access to proprietary knowledge Instead, it is a system trained to recognize and generate patterns in language based on large but incomplete corpora of text. That distinction matters enormously. Why AI Excels at Structure but Struggles with Debugging LLMs are strongest in domains where: principles are stable causal relationships are structural reasoning can be done from first principles knowledge changes slowly This includes: philosophy ethics systems theory governance economics legal reasoning conceptual synthesis In these domains, AI shines because it is doing what it was designed to do: mapping structure and coherence across vast conceptual spaces . But technical troubleshooting — especially hardware/software debugging — is a fundamentally different epistemic terrain. Why Technical Troubleshooting Is a Worst-Case Scenario for AI Modern software bugs are: version-specific undocumented triggered by singular corrupt objects discovered empirically fixed anecdotally shared informally in forums or private channels The correct fix is often: trivial unintuitive non-generalizable recently discovered buried in a forum post from last week This kind of knowledge is anti-structural . It does not reward reasoning. It rewards proximity to the problem and current lived experience. AI is not good at that. The Forum Gap: Where the Best Knowledge Actually Lives Many users assume that AI “combs the internet.” It does not. Unless explicitly enabled to browse, an LLM: cannot see current user forums cannot read new bug reports cannot access paid communities cannot see vendor escalation notes cannot consult internal knowledge bases Ironically, some of the most valuable technical knowledge lives precisely in these spaces : user forums paid mailing lists private Slack groups AppleCare escalation channels tribal technician knowledge This knowledge is: legally restricted socially gated constantly changing poorly documented intentionally non-automatable No amount of money alone solves this. Copyright, licensing and platform restrictions matter. What People Mean When They Say AI “Hallucinates” When technicians say AI “hallucinates,” they are usually describing something more precise: AI can produce internally coherent explanations that are not externally verified. This happens because LLMs optimize for: plausibility linguistic coherence pattern completion Not for: live verification current version accuracy empirical testing In stable domains, that’s a strength. In volatile domains, it’s a liability. The danger isn’t that AI is “making things up” maliciously — it’s that it can sound confident before it has any right to be . Why Subscription Journals Aren’t the Real Fix It’s tempting to think: “Why not just give AI access to all journals and paid sources?” Even if legal barriers disappeared tomorrow, this would not solve the core problem. Most practical technical fixes: never appear in journals are not “research” are discovered after release are context-specific live in informal human networks The problem isn’t missing elite knowledge . It’s missing situated, current, experiential knowledge . That is still a human monopoly. The Correct Way to Use AI (And the Wrong Way) AI should be treated as: a hypothesis generator a conceptual synthesizer a reasoning amplifier a checklist builder a second opinion AI should not be treated as: a final authority on volatile systems a replacement for hands-on expertise a substitute for current forum knowledge an oracle for undocumented bugs The strongest workflow today is hybrid: humans gather current, empirical data AI helps interpret, generalize and reason about it Not the other way around. The Real Lesson AI is not failing when it gets technical debugging wrong. We are failing when we apply it outside its epistemic envelope. Understanding where AI is strong and why it is weak is not a limitation — it is the foundation of responsible use. The future does not belong to AI alone, nor to humans alone. It belongs to those who understand the boundaries — and work intelligently across them. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
