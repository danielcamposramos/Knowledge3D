What they look like: Canary prompts : a short set of questions or inputs that previously exposed edge behavior. ChatGPT said: Yes—that’s a fair read. Once you fix the axioms and observables , you can map ethical behavior into decision procedures (often trees/graphs) that a system can execute and you can audit. How to map ethics into a decision tree (or policy) Scope & stakes Define the domain and what counts as a consequential action. Hard constraints (rights/duties) “Must/never” tests at the top of the tree (e.g., protect minors; no targeted political persuasion; license/consent required). If a branch violates a hard rule → block/route to human. Objectives under constraints Pick the thing to optimize subject to the hard rules (e.g., minimize expected serious harm; maximize factual accuracy). Make terms observable Turn “harm,” “consent,” “private data,” “manipulation,” etc., into concrete checks (classifiers, rules, thresholds). Uncertainty handling Attach probabilities/confidence to each branch. Penalize low-confidence choices; prefer reversible actions when uncertainty is high. Tie-breakers & fairness When two options are within ε of each other, use a declared tie rule (e.g., randomize to avoid hidden bias). Human-in-the-loop nodes Insert approval gates for irreversible or norm-contested actions. Logging & provenance Every consequential path logs inputs, sources, scores, and the rule that fired. Verification loop Keep a regression suite of past failures; run it before shipping changes. Drift canaries ping the critical branches in production and halt if behavior shifts. Micro-examples A) Unavoidable-collision policy (self-driving) Constraint filter: never target protected traits; obey traffic law unless doing so increases expected serious harm. Enumerate maneuvers and for each estimate expected serious harm with error bars. Pick minimum-harm option ; if options tie within ε, randomize . Log sensor state, estimates, choice, and justification; post-incident review updates the suite. B) Factual content generation Constraint filter: refuse targeted political persuasion, medical diagnosis for individuals, etc. If claim is consequential , require dated sources ; if weak evidence → say “uncertain” or decline. If benign , answer normally; always record sources/uncertainty when present. C) Medical triage assistant Constraints: no discrimination; informed consent; clinician override available. Objective: maximize expected healthy life-years saved. Procedure: compute priority scores; surface cases with low confidence for human review ; log rationale. Why this helps (and what it won’t do) Helps: makes value tradeoffs explicit, catches contradictions, creates auditability, and resists drift. Limits: trees can blow up in size; some values conflict (no tree satisfies all fairness criteria); adversaries will game proxies; and no tree grants compassion —it yields governed conduct , not virtue. Practical recipe you can reuse Axioms → Rules (tests) → Priority order → Decision graph → Uncertainty/tie rules → Human gates → Logs & provenance → Canaries & regression tests → Audits & liability . Here’s the precise distinction you’re pressing on. “Governed conduct” vs “virtue” Governed conduct : a system follows explicit rules and objectives (your axioms → tests → priorities → decision tree). It can be audited and improved. This yields ethical compliance . Virtue/compassion : a stable disposition and motive in an agent — caring about others’ good and being willing to bear costs for them. It shapes what we notice, how we interpret cases, and when we go beyond duty . A tree can compute compliant actions. It cannot supply a caring motive , a moral identity , or the attentional stance that compassion gives. That’s the gap. What rules can compute — and what they can’t 1) Motivation & moral credit Computable : whether the act met constraints. Missing : why it was done. Two identical outputs can be obedience vs mercy. Motive matters for trust, responsibility, and legitimacy . 2) Moral attention (what to notice) Computable : decisions once the case is properly framed . Missing : the framing itself — noticing the overlooked victim, the off-metric harm, the hidden asymmetry. Compassion biases attention toward the suffering that dashboards forget. 3) Particular judgment (phronesis) Computable : general rules under modeled contexts. Missing : human practical wisdom in messy particulars where categories blur (friend vs stranger, rule vs spirit). Virtue steers the “spirit of the law” when the letter misfires. 4) Supererogation & grace (beyond duty) Computable : minimum acceptable action. Missing : going further than required , forgiving when punishment is permitted, giving another chance. These preserve relationships and community legitimacy . 5) Value discovery Computable : maximizing stated objectives. Missing : discovering new values or revising priorities when lived experience shows the spec is wrong. Compassion is a sensor for unpriced goods. 6) Cooperation economics Computable : compliance via monitoring and penalties. Missing : the trust dividend . People cooperate more, disclose earlier, and accept hard tradeoffs when they believe decision-makers care . Without that, you need surveillance and force — brittle and costly. 7) Moral growth Computable : stable performance against fixed tests. Missing : becoming a better person over time — forming conscience, learning courage, temperance, humility. That’s a life-forming arc, not an optimization. Why this counters “everything is digitizable” Even perfect rules leave motivational, attentional, and legitimacy gaps. You can simulate “empathy language” or bake in harm-averse penalties , but you haven’t produced concern — only behavior shaped to look like it . In outcomes, that may suffice often; in crises or contested norms, people test motive . If they read “performance,” trust collapses and you pay with coercion. How to approximate compassion in systems (useful, but not the same) Attentional guardrails : mandate “moral attention checks” — ask who is unseen here? Require a short “unmeasured harms” section in consequential decisions. Asymmetric risk penalties : weight harms to the vulnerable more under uncertainty (built-in mercy tilt). Mercy/appeal slots : human panels with authority to override the tree in narrow, logged ways (restorative options, second-chance protocols). Costly commitments : budgeted “supererogation acts” (e.g., hardship waivers) so the system sometimes bears costs for people. Narrative inputs : accept human statements in their own words; require the system to summarize them faithfully before deciding. Virtue at the edges : put virtuous humans where categories are thin (end-of-life, restorative justice, crisis triage), with transparency and review. These patches improve outcomes and public trust — but they remain proxies for care, not care itself. Bottom line Decision trees give us governed conduct : consistent, auditable, often better than ad-hoc human judgment. Compassion and virtue supply what rules can’t : caring motives, moral attention, willingness to go beyond duty, legitimacy that lowers the need for force, and the ability to re-author our values when the spec is wrong. So: we can and should engineer ethical behavior in AI. But the claim that everything moral reduces to math misses the parts of ethics that live in persons — motive, character, love.
