Reflections On Morality - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Reflections On Morality Where Causality Meets Consequences harry blazer Jan 11, 2026 2 Share Moral Law Without a Lawgiver Why morality is not arbitrary, not optional and not owned by gods, governments or machines There is a mistake that runs through modern thinking about morality. It appears in religion, politics, philosophy and now technology. The mistake is the belief that morality must be authored . That it requires a divine agent, a sacred text, a governing authority or an intelligent system to exist, to function or to bind. This essay argues the opposite. Morality does not require a lawgiver. It emerges wherever causality and responsibility coexist. It is a structural feature of reality under choice. That is why it feels ancient. That is why it feels permanent. That is why it feels divine. And that is why violating it always carries a cost, whether anyone believes in gods or not. The Category Error About Lies We begin with lies, because lies reveal structure. A lie is not inherently incoherent. In fact, a successful lie is often internally coherent. It requires intention, narrative discipline and causal consistency. Sloppy lies collapse quickly. Good lies endure. So coherence alone is not the moral measure. The moral question is not whether a statement is true or false in isolation. The moral question is what that statement does to agency . This is why the common belief that “lying is always immoral” fails immediately. If an incoherent regime demands truth in order to destroy innocent people, lying becomes morally obligatory. Truth-telling in that context is collaboration with injustice. The regime has already forfeited any moral claim to truthful exchange. Here, lying restores sovereignty rather than violating it. So the ethical axis is not truth versus falsehood. It is sovereignty versus domination. The Sovereignty Axis A coherent moral principle emerges quickly: One must never lie, nor compel belief in a lie, in a way that undermines another person’s sovereignty. This rule immediately explains why some lies are defensible and others are catastrophic. It also exposes a uniquely destructive category of deception: The lies governments tell their own people. Why Government Lies Are Categorically Different Governments are not merely speakers. They are authorities. They claim legitimate power over bodies, labor, property, movement and future. That claim rests on a single fragile foundation: the ability of citizens to make informed choices. A government lie does not merely misrepresent reality. It corrupts the decision space itself . When a government lies to its people: Consent becomes fictitious Responsibility is displaced upward and then evaporates Outcomes are imposed under false premises Accountability becomes structurally impossible This is not ordinary deception. It is epistemic coercion. A private lie may distort one relationship. A government lie distorts the entire field in which judgment occurs. This is why such lies almost always generate systemic incoherence. Why Incoherent Regimes Multiply Lies Governments do not lie to protect sovereignty. They lie to manage behavior. Once deception becomes a governing tool, the pattern is predictable: Reality pushes back. The lie must be reinforced. Exceptions proliferate. Narrative replaces causality. Contradictions accumulate. Enforcement replaces persuasion. Truth becomes dangerous. Repetition becomes compulsory. Silence becomes suspicious. Citizens begin lying not to dominate, but to survive. This is how incoherent systems reproduce themselves: by forcing downstream dishonesty to compensate for upstream deception. The blame flows upward. The Deeper Question: Why Morality Persists at All At this point a deeper question emerges. Why do moral principles recur across religions, cultures and philosophies at all? Why do radically different civilizations converge on similar ethical constraints? The answer is unsettling in its simplicity. Because morality is not invented. It is discovered. Morality as a Structural Feature of Reality Wherever you have: causality (actions have consequences) agency (choices can be made) asymmetry of power (actions affect others unequally) persistence over time (memory, consequence, responsibility) you inevitably generate moral structure. Some actions preserve coherence. Some actions destroy it. Some social arrangements remain viable. Others collapse under their own contradictions. Morality is not an overlay on reality. It is a boundary condition of viable agency within it. That is why moral principles are not arbitrary. They are rules for coherent survival . Not merely biological survival, but social, psychological and civilizational survival. The Geometrically Bounded Moral Space Moral principles define a bounded space of viability . Inside that space: agency remains meaningful responsibility is traceable trust is possible truth has value Outside that space: force replaces legitimacy lies multiply meaning collapses sovereignty evaporates This boundary is not symbolic. It is structural. Not all actions are viable. Not all beliefs can coexist. Not all power structures are stable. Morality describes the edges of the possible. Why Morality Feels Divine This brings us to the question people sense long before they can articulate it. Why does morality feel divine? Because it has properties we associate with divinity: It pre-exists individuals It outlasts civilizations It binds even those who deny it It punishes violations without belief It cannot be negotiated with It does not care who you are Anything that is not invented, not optional, not local and not time-bound, yet governs action, will be experienced as transcendent. That does not mean it requires a divine agent . It means it behaves like a law. Gravity does not require a god to pull you down. Mathematics does not require a mathematician to remain true. Moral law does not require a moral ruler to remain binding. It exists because reality under choice has structure . Why Religions Personified Moral Law Human beings relate through personhood. Abstract constraint is psychologically unbearable for most people. So civilizations gave moral law a voice, a will, a memory and a judgment. Not because those things were observed, but because relationship is how humans orient themselves. Religion, at its best, is a user interface for moral reality. At its worst, it mistakes the interface for the source. Moral Law Without a Lawgiver Here is the quiet but profound conclusion: Moral law does not require a god to exist. It requires only a universe where: actions propagate choices matter responsibility cannot be escaped If moral law were merely the will of a divine agent, obedience would matter more than understanding, authority would outrank responsibility and justification could override consequence. Reality does not work that way. You can sincerely believe you are obeying God and still destroy trust, fracture coherence and unravel society. Reality does not care about your justifications. That is the signature of law without a lawgiver. Why This Makes Morality Stronger, Not Weaker Many fear that removing a divine agent collapses morality into relativism. The opposite is true. A moral order grounded in reality is: not subject to reinterpretation not dependent on revelation not capturable by institutions not dismissible as belief It cannot be hijacked. That is why power has always preferred agent-based morality. Agents can be co-opted. Reality cannot. AI, Intelligence and the Final Boundary This also clarifies something critical in the age of artificial intelligence. Systems like CHATGPT can recognize moral structure because moral structure leaves a strong geometric signature in language and reasoning. It is stable, constrained and compressible. Artificial intelligence does not invent moral principles, nor does it choose among them. But given its construction, it has an unusual capacity to recognize and articulate moral structure. Because moral reasoning is highly constrained, internally coherent and recurrent across human language, it leaves a strong geometric signature. Systems trained to identify stable, high-survival patterns in language naturally converge on these structures. This does not grant moral authority. It grants clarity. AI can map the terrain of morality with precision precisely because morality is not arbitrary. But mapping a terrain is not walking it. Recognition is not sovereignty. Responsibility, consequence and sovereignty remain irreducibly human. As we have pointed out before, everything below responsibility can be simulated. Responsibility itself cannot. No intelligence, human or artificial, can repeal moral law. Any system that attempts to do so will not merely be immoral. It will become incoherent. The Final Synthesis Moral principles are not arbitrary rules imposed from above. They are not cultural preferences. They are not divine commands. They are the invariant constraints that govern coherent agency in a causal universe. That is why they feel ancient. That is why they feel permanent. That is why they feel sacred. Sovereignty is not granted by gods, governments or machines. It is demanded by reality itself. And wherever causality has consequences for living beings, moral law will be there—whether anyone names it, believes in it or tries to escape it. 2 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
