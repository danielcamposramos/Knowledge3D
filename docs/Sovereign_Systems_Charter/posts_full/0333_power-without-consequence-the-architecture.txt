Introduction Modern civilization has entered an era where power that immensely damages society can be exercised with little or no consequence. Across government, corporate, technological, and financial domains, we are witnessing a systematic decoupling of power from accountability . This transformation is not accidental — it is codified in law, embedded in governance structures, and reinforced by narratives of progress, safety, and innovation. When those who create harm are protected from its effects, we have replaced justice with insulation. What follows is an exploration of how this syndrome has been constructed and how it now defines our age. II. Legal Precedents: The State-Crafted Escape Hatch A. Price-Anderson Act (1957): To enable the development of private nuclear energy, the U.S. Congress passed the Price-Anderson Act — shielding nuclear companies from full liability in the event of a catastrophic accident. The cost of failure was effectively transferred to the public. The result was the normalization of profit without risk . B. Vaccine Injury Act (1986) and PREP Act: Facing growing legal challenges from vaccine-related injuries, Congress created the Vaccine Injury Compensation Program. This acknowledged that harm could occur but removed liability from pharmaceutical companies. In fact, the Supreme Court ruled that vaccines are inherently dangerous and harmful. The industry was being overwhelmed by damage claims so legislators decided to make it so that the industry wouldn’t be damaged by the damage their products caused, so they could remain profitable making the products causing the damage!!! The PREP Act extended this logic even further during public health emergencies — creating total corporate immunity under certain conditions. While millions of people were damaged by the products, the industry enjoyed record revenues and profitability. C. Intelligence Authorizations and the National Security State: Since the Cold War, laws like the Intelligence Authorization Act, FISA and the State Secrets Privilege have enabled intelligence agencies to act covertly and unaccountably . Operations like MK-Ultra, COINTELPRO and mass surveillance programs reveal a parallel system of governance beyond democratic oversight. FASAB 56* compounds the problem of lack of accountability in spades (see explanation at end of commentary). D. Legal Protections for Civil Servants and Government Officials: In addition to shielding corporations and covert agencies, the legal system also provides qualified immunity and broad protections for civil servants and government officials . While originally intended to allow these actors to perform their duties without fear of frivolous lawsuits, such protections have increasingly been interpreted to allow egregious misconduct to go unpunished. Police, regulatory agents, military personnel, and bureaucrats may act with near-total immunity, so long as they can invoke institutional backing or national security rationale. III. Finance: Socialized Risk, Privatized Reward The 2008 financial crisis exposed the extent to which power had been divorced from responsibility. &quot;Too Big to Fail&quot; banks were bailed out after engaging in reckless and often fraudulent behavior. No senior executives were meaningfully prosecuted. The system survived — but the moral architecture of risk collapsed. IV. Tech and AI: Synthetic Power, Zero Liability A. Section 230 Tech platforms exercise unprecedented control over speech, identity, commerce and visibility — but under Section 230, they bear no legal responsibility for the content they host or suppress. This immunity has allowed them to become unelected arbiters of discourse. B. AI Development and Preemptive Immunity: AI companies are already seeking legal insulation from harms their models may cause — ranging from disinformation to algorithmic bias to autonomous systems failure. The push echoes earlier strategies in pharma and nuclear energy: unleash high-risk tech, then demand protection from its consequences. C. Rights Inverted: From Liberty to Compliance A profound inversion has taken place in the legal hierarchy: Your constitutional rights (free speech, privacy, due process) can be ignored by tech platforms and outsourced state functions. But your civil rights (race, gender, age discrimination) are tightly enforced — because they regulate social behavior, not institutional power. You can be banned from social media for expressing constitutionally protected opinions, yet those platforms cannot discriminate against you based on your race. This reveals a deeper shift: rights are no longer about protecting sovereignty — they are about managing identity within acceptable bounds . V. Governance by Obscurity: Intelligence as a Fourth Branch Modern intelligence agencies wield sovereign-level power with almost no public accountability. Legislative frameworks like the National Security Act of 1947 and endless emergency powers allow operations to proceed in secrecy, shielded from both the public and most elected officials. Plausible deniability becomes policy. Oversight becomes theater. VI. Consequences of Power Without Accountability Ethical paralysis among leaders and institutions Demoralization of the public Systemic harm with no legal or moral feedback loop Collapse of causal logic in governance and justice VII. Reuniting Power and Consequence: Restoring a free and moral civilization requires that we realign power with accountability. Proposals include: Personal liability for corporate and institutional actors Radical transparency in intelligence and AI development Sunsets on liability shields Reaffirmation of constitutional primacy over regulatory convenience Cultural renewal of sovereignty as responsibility Conclusion: The separation of power from consequence is the signature pathology of our time. It underlies our political dysfunction, our technological risks, and our loss of trust. Rebuilding the moral architecture of civilization means restoring the sacred link between what we do and what we must answer for . Without it, power becomes not only dangerous — it becomes inhuman. * What Is FASAB Statement 56? FASAB Statement No. 56 , titled “Classified Activities” , was issued by the Federal Accounting Standards Advisory Board (FASAB) on October 4, 2018 . It allows federal government agencies to modify or omit financial disclosures and statements in publicly available accounting documents if those disclosures are deemed to endanger national security. 🔒 In short: It legalizes secret or altered accounting practices in the name of national defense or security. 📘 Key Provisions of FASAB 56 Modified Reporting Agencies can modify or exclude financial information that would normally be required under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) if disclosure would harm national security. Classified Sources and Methods If revealing financial transactions would expose intelligence methods, partnerships, contractors, or technologies, agencies are now empowered to omit or misrepresent that information. Consolidated Reporting Deception Treasury and OMB are permitted to include classified components in ways that are not distinguishable from unclassified reporting — potentially misleading Congress and the public . No Audit Trail Required There is no requirement to disclose that information has been modified or omitted. This means a completely false picture can be painted in official financial reports, with no legal obligation to acknowledge the changes. 🏛 Who Created It? FASAB is a little-known government body that sets accounting standards for federal agencies. It includes representatives from: The Treasury Department The Office of Management and Budget (OMB) The General Accounting Office (GAO) Independent members from the accounting industry ⚠️ Why It’s Controversial Destroys financial transparency in federal budgeting — even to Congress and oversight bodies Legalizes “black budgets” and untraceable spending , including potential unauthorized or off-book military/intelligence operations Undermines traditional audit requirements and makes accountability impossible Raises fears of unconstitutional or extra-legal behavior hidden behind &quot;classified&quot; labels 📌 Related Concepts “Dark money” budgeting or “black budget” accounting FASAB 56 applies only to federal agencies , but its impact ripples through government contractors and entities tied to national security Not to be confused with FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) which governs private sector accounting harry’s Substack is a reader-supported publication.
