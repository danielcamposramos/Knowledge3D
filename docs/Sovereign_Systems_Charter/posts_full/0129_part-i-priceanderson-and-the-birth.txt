PART I Price–Anderson and the Birth of Legalized Moral Hazard harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in PART I Price–Anderson and the Birth of Legalized Moral Hazard How nuclear power taught the modern state to socialize catastrophic risk harry blazer Jan 04, 2026 3 Share There is a widespread belief that corporate immunity is a late-stage corruption of law—an aberration born of modern lobbying excess. That belief is comforting. It is also wrong. The modern doctrine of legally sanctioned risk externalization was not invented by pharmaceutical companies, nor by Silicon Valley, nor even by modern finance. It was perfected in the nuclear industry. And it required not just engineers and politicians, but elite legal minds capable of converting uninsurable danger into statutory normalcy. The Nuclear Problem No One Could Solve Nuclear technology posed a dilemma unlike anything industry had previously encountered. Not because it was dangerous—industry routinely handles danger—but because its failure modes were infinite in scale and duration . A nuclear accident could: Render land unusable for generations Create harm without temporal boundaries Produce damages no balance sheet could absorb Exceed any private insurance capacity The private insurance market understood this immediately. It refused to insure nuclear risk. That refusal was the moment when the trajectory of modern law quietly changed. DuPont and the Condition for Participation When the United States embarked on the nuclear age—first militarily, then commercially—it turned to DuPont not as a supplier, but as a systems architect . DuPont had already: Designed and operated plutonium production at Hanford Site Built and managed the chemical separation backbone of the bomb Created industrial-scale radioactive processes from nothing But DuPont also understood something the public did not: If liability were unlimited, no rational corporation would touch nuclear technology. DuPont’s participation came with a condition—explicit, non-negotiable, and foundational: The federal government would assume ultimate liability. This was not greed. It was actuarial reality and self-preservation. Price–Anderson: When Law Became the Insurer The solution was the Price-Anderson Act of 1957. Price–Anderson did something unprecedented: It capped total industry liability It pooled private operator contributions It made the federal government the backstop It shifted catastrophic risk to the public It enabled private participation in an otherwise uninsurable industry This was not deregulation. It was legal engineering . The Lawyers Behind the Curtain This transformation did not happen by accident. It required: Lawyers who understood insurance law Lawyers fluent in administrative statecraft Lawyers capable of drafting statutes that sounded like public protection while functioning as industry enablement The role of elite law firms here was not adversarial. It was architectural . They: Translated actuarial impossibility into statutory necessity Reframed liability as a national security issue Designed compensation mechanisms that precluded jury adjudication Embedded immunity inside regulatory compliance From this point forward, immunity would substitute for accountability . This was the first successful proof that: If an industry is declared essential enough, law can be made to absorb its worst failures. The Precedent No One Names Price–Anderson did more than enable nuclear power. It established a template : Declare an activity strategically indispensable Acknowledge that private markets cannot insure it Cap liability by statute Socialize catastrophic risk Preserve private expertise and control This template did not remain confined to reactors. It migrated. Why Nuclear Was the Perfect Test Case Nuclear power succeeded as the prototype because: The risk was invisible The science was inaccessible The fear was abstract The benefits were framed as existential The public could not meaningfully consent because it could not meaningfully assess. That asymmetry persists to this day. The Moral Pivot Point At the moment Price–Anderson passed, the United States made a quiet moral decision: Some harms are too large to assign responsibility for. Once that principle is accepted, the only remaining question becomes: Which industries qualify? The Long Shadow Today: Nuclear cleanup costs persist for generations Original contractors are insulated Knowledge remains private Harm remains public This is not an indictment of nuclear physics. It is an indictment of a legal doctrine that separated power from consequence. Why This Matters Now The nuclear industry did not merely generate electricity or weapons. It generated a jurisprudence of immunity . That jurisprudence now underwrites: Pharmaceutical liability shields Emergency powers Chemical externalities Digital governance without recourse The war for sovereignty and soul , did not begin with algorithms. It began when law learned how to make catastrophic harm administratively tolerable. Price–Anderson did not solve the nuclear liability problem. It taught modern power how to survive accountability. 3 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
