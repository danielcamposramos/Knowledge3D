AI Medicine and the End of Discretion - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in AI Medicine and the End of Discretion When Blind Spots Are Automated harry blazer Jan 03, 2026 2 Share Modern medicine already struggles with a fundamental limitation: it is a statistical enterprise attempting to care for individual bodies. That problem, while serious, has historically been mitigated by one crucial factor — human discretion . Clinicians hesitate. They override protocols. They listen to patients. They sometimes say, “This doesn’t feel right.” As medicine becomes increasingly mediated by AI, algorithms and protocol automation, that last safeguard is quietly disappearing. And with it, something essential is being lost. From Statistical Judgment to Algorithmic Authority AI in medicine is often presented as a neutral improvement: better data, fewer errors, more consistency. And in many narrow contexts, that is true. But consistency is not wisdom. AI systems are trained on: Population datasets Coded diagnoses Institutional outcomes Billing and compliance structures They learn what works on average , under defined conditions , for most people . They do not learn: Individual thresholds Early subjective signals Contextual fragility Biological idiosyncrasy What statistical medicine treated as an unfortunate limitation, AI medicine treats as noise to be filtered out . The Fate of the Outlier In traditional medicine, the outlier is inconvenient but visible. In algorithmic medicine, the outlier is a model error . AI systems are optimized to: Reduce variance Increase confidence Standardize decisions Minimize deviation From the system’s point of view, a sensitive individual is not a warning — they are a false positive. The irony is stark: The very people who register harm first are the people the system is most likely to dismiss. Automation Removes Hesitation A human clinician can pause. An algorithm cannot. Once protocols are automated: Deviations require justification Compliance becomes safer than judgment Liability shifts toward following the model Clinical intuition becomes a risk factor The “standard of care” stops being a guideline and becomes a rail . And rails are not designed for steering. The Illusion of Personalized AI Medicine Much will be made of “personalized medicine” powered by AI. In practice, this usually means: Finer stratification More demographic buckets Better cohort matching This is not personalization. It is classification with higher resolution . True individuality is not static. It is dynamic, historical and context-dependent. It cannot be inferred reliably from population data, no matter how large the dataset or sophisticated the model. AI can recognize patterns. It cannot recognize you . When Blind Spots Are Automated Here is the crux: When statistical medicine becomes automated, its blind spots become automated as well. What was once an acknowledged limitation becomes embedded in infrastructure. What could once be overridden becomes enforced by default. What could once be questioned becomes invisible. This is not malevolent. It is mechanical. But mechanical systems do not forgive edge cases. The Sovereignty Collision Individual sovereignty depends on: The legitimacy of subjective experience The right to refuse protocol The authority to act on early signals The freedom to deviate without penalty Algorithmic medicine depends on: Predictability Standardization Auditability Compliance These are not morally opposed. They are structurally incompatible at scale . When conflict arises, the system will resolve it the only way it can: by delegitimizing the individual signal. What Is Actually at Stake This is not a debate about technology. It is a question of where authority resides . AI can be a tool. AI must not become an arbiter. The moment algorithmic output overrides lived biological experience, medicine ceases to be care and becomes administration. Efficient, scalable, defensible — and incomplete. The Necessary Boundary If AI is to be used ethically in medicine, certain principles must be non-negotiable: Human discretion must remain protected Protocols must be defeasible, not binding Early subjective signals must retain legitimacy Opt-out pathways must exist without penalty Without these, medicine will become more coherent — and less humane. Final Thought The promise of AI medicine is optimization. The danger is forgetting what is being optimized for . A system can be statistically correct and individually lethal. A model can be accurate and still erase the human it fails to see. The future of medicine will not be decided by algorithms alone — but by whether individuals are still allowed to say: “This may be safe on average, but it is not safe for me.” If that sentence becomes unsayable, sovereignty will have been lost. 2 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
