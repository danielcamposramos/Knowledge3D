It is a statistical discipline , optimized for populations, averages and risk distributions. This distinction matters far more than most people realize — especially for those whose bodies do not conform neatly to the mean. Medicine, as currently practiced, does not treat you . It treats “ someones” statistically similar to you (height, weight, sex, age etc…) That difference is not semantic. It is structural. Medicine as Population Management Clinical medicine is built on randomized controlled trials, population cohorts and probabilistic risk reduction. These tools are powerful, indispensable and have saved countless lives. But they also come with a built-in blind spot: Averages do not describe people. They describe distributions. When a doctor prescribes a medication, the dosage is not calibrated to your redox balance, metabolic reserve, inflammatory baseline, lifetime toxic load or signaling sensitivity. It is calibrated to a statistical envelope deemed “safe enough” for most people, most of the time. This is not negligence. It is necessity. Medicine must function in a world of limited time, incomplete data, legal exposure, and heterogeneous patients. So it does what large systems always do: it optimizes for risk minimization , not individual precision. The Speed Limit Analogy Think of the speed limit. A speed limit is not: The optimal speed for your car The safest speed for your skill level The appropriate speed for current road and weather conditions It is set to protect the lowest common denominator under the worst plausible conditions . Medicine works the same way. Dosage guidelines, treatment protocols and “standard of care” are speed limits. They are designed to prevent catastrophe across a broad population, not to maximize outcomes (or minimize damage) for a particular individual. If you can safely drive faster, the system does not care. If you are harmed driving slower, the system shrugs. Sensitivity Is Misunderstood People who react strongly to medications, supplements or interventions are often labeled as “sensitive,” “idiosyncratic” or worse — anxious or psychosomatic. This framing is backwards. In many cases, sensitivity reflects: Lower background biochemical noise Fewer compensatory buffers masking perturbation Higher signal-to-noise ratio in physiological feedback A “cleaner” system registers disturbances earlier. A more burdened system absorbs insult longer — until it collapses. What looks like fragility may actually be precision . The canary in the coal mine is not weak. It is responsive. Threshold Biology vs Linear Thinking Biological systems do not fail linearly. They fail at thresholds . Damage accumulates quietly until a tipping point is crossed, after which effects appear suddenly and irreversibly. This is true for cataracts, neurodegeneration, autoimmune disease, metabolic collapse and countless other conditions. Statistical medicine struggles with thresholds because thresholds are individual . A five-day drug exposure may be irrelevant for one person and decisive for another. Not because one is imagining effects, but because their internal systems are closer to an edge the model cannot see. Medicine notices damage only after it becomes visible. The individual often experiences it before that point. The Sovereignty Gap Here is where this becomes a sovereignty issue, not just a medical one. If sovereignty means responsibility for one’s own body, choices and consequences, then medicine — by design — operates below the sovereignty layer. It cannot know your internal state. It cannot feel your early warning signals. It cannot validate subjective experience until it becomes objective pathology. So the burden returns to the individual: To notice inflection points To decline interventions that exceed their tolerance To accept responsibility for deviating from protocol This is not anti-medicine. It is post-naïve medicine . The Quiet Failure of Informed Consent Informed consent presumes: Known risks Predictable responses Meaningful probabilities But statistical risk is not personal risk. When medicine says, “This is very unlikely,” what it really means is: “Most people did not report this in trials.” It does not mean: You will not experience it Your system will tolerate it Your threshold has been assessed True informed consent would require acknowledging something medicine is not built to do: It cannot model you . A Necessary Reframing None of this is an argument against medicine. It is an argument against confusing population safety with individual safety , and against dismissing early signals simply because they fall outside the bell curve. The future of medicine, if it is to be ethical as well as effective, must grapple with this truth: Statistical coherence does not guarantee individual completeness. Until it does, individual sovereignty remains not a luxury, but a necessity. Final Thought The most dangerous phrase in modern healthcare is not “experimental.” It is “within normal limits.” Normal for whom? The speed limit keeps traffic flowing. But survival still depends on the driver knowing their own vehicle.
