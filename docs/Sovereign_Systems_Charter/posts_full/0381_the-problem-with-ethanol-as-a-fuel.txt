Subscribe ChatGPT said: You're essentially correct — modern engines not specifically designed or tuned for ethanol will perform best, in terms of engine longevity and performance, on ethanol-free gasoline (often called “pure gas” or E0). Here’s a breakdown to clarify: ✅ Ethanol vs. Non-Ethanol in Modern Engines FactorEthanol-Blended Gas (e.g., E10, E15)Pure Gasoline (E0)Engine Wear Slightly more corrosive; absorbs waterLess corrosive; more stable Fuel Efficiency Lower energy content (~3-5% less MPG with E10)Higher energy content = better MPG Combustion Burns cleaner (less CO/HC emissions)Slightly more carbon output Compatibility E10 is compatible with nearly all modern engines (post-2001); E15 only with post-2007 EPA-approved modelsFully compatible with all gasoline engines Long-Term Effects Can degrade rubber, plastics, and gaskets over time (especially in older vehicles or small engines)More gentle on engine components over time ⚠️ Key Points: E10 is safe for most post-2001 engines , and most late-model vehicles are flex-fuel tolerant to some degree. E15 is only EPA-approved for 2001+ model years , but not universally recommended by manufacturers. Ethanol has lower energy density than gasoline — meaning less power and fuel economy per gallon. Ethanol can attract and absorb water , which can lead to phase separation and potential fuel system corrosion if fuel sits for extended periods (especially in marine engines or seasonal equipment). If your vehicle's manufacturer does not explicitly approve E15 or E85 , pure gasoline is generally better for: Performance Fuel economy Engine longevity 🔧 Bottom Line: While E10 is usually fine for late-model engines (and legally required in many regions), if you have access to ethanol-free gasoline and you’re concerned with maximizing engine life and performance , then pure gasoline is indeed superior . The engine may tolerate ethanol — but it won’t prefer it. Let me know if you want a breakdown for small engines (lawnmowers, boats, etc.) or performance cars — the ethanol impact is more pronounced there. ChatGPT said: Yes — your strategy of using ethanol-free 89 octane and adding a quality octane booster to reach 93 is valid and can work well for older V8 engines that need higher octane fuel but would benefit from avoiding ethanol. Here's a clear breakdown: ✅ Why Use Ethanol-Free + Octane Booster? Ethanol Content 0% – avoids water absorption, phase separation, corrosion~10% – slightly corrosive, can degrade older seals Fuel Energy Content Higher BTUs per gallon (ethanol-free is more energy dense)Lower energy per gallon (ethanol reduces MPG) Engine Impact (Older V8) Gentler on older seals, valves, and fuel linesEthanol can degrade older components not designed for it Storage Stability Much longer shelf life — better for seasonal useEthanol attracts water and breaks down faster ⚠️ Things to Keep in Mind: Octane Boosters Vary in Quality : Many cheap “octane boosters” only raise octane by fractions of a point. Use a well-reviewed, lab-tested product like Royal Purple Max Boost , Torco Accelerator , or Lucas Octane Booster . These can raise 89 to 93+ if dosed correctly. Mixing Accuracy : Follow exact mixing ratios. Over-boosting can leave unburned additives, while under-boosting can cause detonation (pinging). Some people use toluene or xylene (solvents with high octane ratings) as DIY boosters — effective but risky if not blended properly. Cost Consideration : The combined cost of ethanol-free gas + quality booster might exceed premium pump fuel. But if engine health, longevity, and avoiding ethanol are your priority, it’s worth it. Check Your Compression Ratio : If your engine has a high compression ratio (e.g., 10:1 or above), staying at 93+ is critical to avoid knocking. If compression is slightly lower, you may tolerate ~91 octane without damage — but older V8s with performance tuning often need the full 93. 🔧 Bottom Line: Yes — your approach is solid if you use a proven octane booster and need to avoid ethanol for longevity or mechanical reasons. It's common among classic car owners, muscle car drivers, and those with marine or aviation fuel needs. Would you like a list of the most effective octane boosters ranked by lab tests?
