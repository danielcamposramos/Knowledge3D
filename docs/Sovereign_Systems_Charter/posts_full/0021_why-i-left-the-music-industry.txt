Not Enough Art. harry blazer Feb 10, 2026 Share For a long time, music was where special meaning happened for me. Not sound, not performance, not career — meaning. It happened in rooms and venues where musicians listened to one another, reacted in real time, took chances together and allowed something unplanned to emerge. The music was not the notes. It was the interaction. And then, gradually, that center disappeared. When opportunity was replaced by process One moment stands out. I was asked by someone I respected to play drums on a recording session featuring his instrumental work. When I arrived, I was the only musician there. This was during the shift in the 1970s, when the industry began laying down rhythm tracks to a click, to be overdubbed later by others in separate sessions. Technically, nothing was wrong. Musically, something essential was gone. What disappeared wasn’t pressure or difficulty. It was opportunity . The opportunity to respond. The opportunity to surprise one another. The opportunity for something greater than the sum of the parts to appear. Music stopped being something that happened between people and became something that was assembled across time . Listening gave way to alignment. Responsiveness gave way to synchronization. We didn’t meet each other in sound anymore — we obeyed an external grid. At that moment, I realized that creativity had been displaced by workflow. When musicians became technicians This change didn’t arrive alone. I watched drugs hollow out extraordinary artists — not in a romantic sense, but in a quietly tragic one. Talent remained, but presence vanished. The music was no longer the master. I watched politics take precedence over art. Positions mattered more than perception. Alignment mattered more than truth. Music became a vehicle rather than a pursuit. I watched major stars with surprisingly shallow musical understanding pass judgment and blame on others with authority they hadn’t earned. The industry rewarded visibility over depth, brand over craft. I watched musicians I respected — people who knew better — stay silent when something was wrong because speaking up threatened their livelihood. I understood the fear. I also understood the cost. What was once a calling became a career management exercise. What was once expression became compliance. And slowly, the role of the musician changed. We didn’t stop being skilled. We stopped being artists. We became technicians — competent, efficient, replaceable. When the emotional core was stripped away But beneath all of this — technology, drugs, politics, hierarchy — something deeper was happening. The emotional core of music (its soul) was being removed. Music is not primarily about sound. It’s about feeling made audible . About laughter, sorrow, longing, joy, memory — not represented, but expressed. Not assembled, but lived. When music is reduced to tracks, grids, samples and overdubs, emotion becomes texture rather than experience. Human presence becomes raw material. This was the beginning of sampling culture — the extraction of human moments to be recombined elsewhere, by others, without the original encounter that gave them meaning. At that point, music didn’t stop existing. But it stopped asking anything of the people making it . And without that mutual asking — that invitation to respond — the art lost its center. Why I left I didn’t leave because the industry changed. Industries always change. I left because the reason for being there disappeared . The thing I loved — the living, shared, unrepeatable experience of making music together — was no longer central. It was optional. Sometimes even inconvenient. What replaced it was scalable, efficient, profitable and empty of the very thing that drew me in. So I stepped away. Because when an art becomes mostly industry and the human encounter becomes secondary, staying requires a kind of quiet dishonesty. And I wasn’t willing to practice that. What remains I still love music. I still recognize it when it happens. It just doesn’t usually happen where the industry lives anymore. The industry didn’t kill music. It displaced it. And I chose not to follow. And this is what it will be like when AI takes over. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
