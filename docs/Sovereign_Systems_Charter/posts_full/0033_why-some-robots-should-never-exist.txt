Why Some Robots Should Never Exist - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Why Some Robots Should Never Exist Prohibited Classes, Sovereignty Boundaries and the Final Bright Line harry blazer Feb 09, 2026 1 Share Every civilization eventually confronts a limit it cannot cross without undoing itself. The embodied age is forcing that moment now. After reaffirming sovereign self-defense and rejecting the fantasy of making autonomous systems “moral,” we arrive at the unavoidable conclusion: Some machines are incompatible with human sovereignty, regardless of safeguards, intentions or claimed necessity. This is not anti-technology. It is boundary-setting — the oldest function of law and ethics. The mistake of technological neutrality A common argument goes like this: “Technology is neutral. Only use matters.” That argument has always been false. Civilizations have always prohibited entire classes of tools, not because of misuse, but because their normal use destroys the conditions for lawful human life . We ban: private nuclear weapons chemical agents biological weapons torture devices certain forms of surveillance Not because they cannot be used carefully, but because their existence collapses moral symmetry and accountability . Robotics now joins that list. The decisive criterion: non-consensual autonomous coercion We do not need a long list of edge cases. We need one clear discriminator. Any system that can apply force, restraint, pursuit or compulsion to a human being without that person’s contemporaneous consent and without accountable human agency is incompatible with sovereignty. Once this line is crossed: consent disappears, due process evaporates, self-defense becomes criminalized, and power decouples from accountability. No amount of logging, auditing or “alignment” repairs that. Prohibited class one: autonomous coercive agents in civil space These systems should never exist outside tightly controlled military contexts, if at all: autonomous patrol robots autonomous law enforcement robots autonomous crowd control systems autonomous pursuit or apprehension machines autonomous weapons deployed among civilians This prohibition applies by capability , not by mission statement. If a system can: pursue individuals, restrain bodies, enforce compliance, decide punishment, apply force without immediate human control, then it does not belong in civil society. A society that normalizes machine coercion forfeits sovereignty by design. Prohibited class two: systems that cannot be refused or exited A robot that cannot be refused is not a service — it is an imposition. Any embodied system that: cannot be locally declined, cannot be escaped without penalty, cannot be physically avoided, cannot be disabled through lawful self-defense, has crossed from assistance into domination. Civil space must preserve the right of exit . Without exit, consent is fictional. Prohibited class three: dual-use platforms designed for latent coercion One of the most dangerous tricks of the embodied age is capability laundering . A system is sold as benign: a delivery robot, a helper, a service platform. But its architecture quietly allows: force amplification, pursuit, weapon attachment, coercive software unlocks. These systems must be treated as coercive from inception . If coercion can be enabled by update, attachment or configuration, then coercion already exists. There is no neutral “potential” when sovereignty is at stake. Why emergency exceptions cannot be allowed History is unequivocal: Every emergency exception becomes permanent. False flag or real crisis, the pattern is the same: suspension of rights, expansion of force, normalization of surveillance, retroactive justification. Autonomous coercive systems are uniquely dangerous here because they: scale instantly, operate continuously, diffuse responsibility, outlast human attention. Therefore: No emergency provision may authorize autonomous coercion in civil space. If human judgment, accountability and restraint cannot be preserved during crisis, the technology must yield — not the Constitution. The difference between governance and domination True governance constrains power. Domination automates it. Robotics tempts societies to replace: deliberation with execution, judgment with optimization, accountability with efficiency. That temptation must be resisted explicitly. A robot cannot: weigh mercy, bear guilt, accept punishment, reconcile with those it harms. When force is exercised without those human burdens, justice ceases to exist. The final test of legitimacy Here is the test every proposed embodied system must pass: If this system were used against you, in error, without recourse, would sovereignty survive? If the honest answer is no, the system does not belong in human society. Progress requires prohibition This is the part technologists resist most, but history confirms it: Progress is not the absence of limits. Progress is the wisdom to choose them. A civilization that cannot say “this shall not be built” has already surrendered its future to momentum. Closing statement Not everything that can be engineered should be permitted to exist. Not every efficiency is worth its cost. Not every problem should be solved by force. Human sovereignty is not negotiable. Robotics must conform to that fact — or be refused entry into the human world. The trilogy, complete The Second Amendment in the Age of Robotics Sovereign self-defense is foundational.
