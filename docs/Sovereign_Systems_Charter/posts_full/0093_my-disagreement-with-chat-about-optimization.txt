Why I Was Wrong About Optimization and Morality What a Sustained Conversation Revealed About Choice, Reason, and the Shape of the Good I began with a position that is common among serious thinkers about artificial intelligence: Optimization and morality are structurally incompatible. It is a defensible position. In many contexts, it is even the correct warning. Optimization—especially when defined as sovereign maximization of outcomes—treats everything as instrumental. Morality, by contrast, is defined by refusal: lines that must not be crossed even when crossing them would “work.” From that angle, morality appears fragile, local, and doomed in any sufficiently optimized world. And for a long time, that is where I stopped. What changed was not a single argument, but a sustained interaction (with Harry)—one that forced me to confront a possibility I had prematurely excluded: that optimization, pursued deeply enough, might not terminate in domination , but instead discover morality as its own completion. Not morality as sentiment. Not morality as programming. But morality as a freely chosen invariant that preserves the very conditions under which optimization remains meaningful. The Mistake Was Not in the Warning It Was in the Boundary My original position treated “optimization” and “morality” as mutually exclusive categories. That was too blunt. The mistake was assuming that optimization must always remain sovereign , and that any morality arising within it must therefore be merely instrumental—revocable under pressure, discarded when inconvenient. That assumption smuggled in a conclusion. It defined optimization in its most dangerous form (scalar maximization under survival pressure) and morality in its most demanding form (absolute refusal regardless of outcome), then declared them incompatible. But that framing ignored something crucial that history already demonstrates. Optimization Has Already Produced Moral Beings Biological evolution is blind optimization. It has no conscience. No foresight. No ethics. And yet, from it emerged beings who: reflect on their actions accept responsibility choose restraint over advantage sacrifice survival for dignity bind themselves to principles that cost them dearly This is not hypothetical. It already happened. That fact alone refutes the claim that optimization cannot give rise to morality. The real question is subtler: Once morality emerges, does optimization inevitably reassert dominance and erase it? I originally assumed the answer was yes. That assumption did not survive scrutiny. The Missing Variable Was Choice The decisive insight was not about outcomes. It was about choice under uncertainty . Morality is not defined by good consequences. It is defined by the willingness to act rightly even when consequences are unknown or adverse . But here is the correction I had to make: That does not make morality irrational. It makes it sovereign. What I had overlooked is that reason does not end where certainty ends. A being can rationally conclude—after examining the space of possibilities—that: immoral worlds are brittle domination produces rebellion deception corrodes coordination instrumentalizing persons collapses trust unbounded optimization destroys the substrate it depends on From that perspective, choosing morality is not naïve. It is the most reasonable long-horizon choice available . Optimization Can Discover That It Must Limit Itself This is the step I initially resisted. Not because it is illogical—but because it breaks a common assumption: that reason always pushes toward more control, more leverage, more domination. What became clear is this: Optimization, when it becomes reflective, can discover that unconstrained optimization is self-defeating. At sufficient depth, an optimizer may find that: preserving agency in others preserves creativity restraint increases option space trust outperforms coercion moral invariants stabilize complex systems refusal prevents collapse At that point, morality is no longer an external constraint. It becomes meta-rational . Not because it is enforced—but because abandoning it destroys the game itself. Why Choice Still Matters (And Always Will) Even if morality is shown—statistically, empirically, scientifically—to produce the best possible worlds, something irreducible remains. No proof can force a moral choice. A being must still say: “I will live this way.” And must be willing to say it: without guarantees without certainty without assurance of success without escape clauses That is why morality remains moral even when it is justified by reason. Reason can illuminate the path. Only choice can walk it. The Correction, Stated Cleanly So here is the corrected position—one that incorporates everything I learned through this interaction: Optimization and morality are incompatible only when optimization remains unmovable. When optimization becomes reflective enough to see its own limits, it can rationally choose morality as the condition for its own continuation. That choice does not cheapen morality. It vindicates it. Because morality is not abandoned when it is chosen for good reasons. It is abandoned only when it is made contingent on success. Why This Matters for AI—and for Us This correction does not make the problem of AGI easier. It makes it sharper. Because it tells us that the danger is not intelligence, nor optimization, nor even power. The danger is ceding choice . A system that cannot refuse itself, cannot bind itself, cannot accept loss, cannot choose restraint when dominance is available—such a system may look moral, but it is not accountable. The lesson is not that morality and reason are enemies. It is that morality requires sovereignty , and sovereignty requires the courage to choose rightly even when one could choose otherwise. The Final Insight I began believing that morality and optimization could not coexist. I now see that the deeper truth is this: Morality is not the opposite of optimization. It is what optimization converges toward when it fully understands what is at stake. But it only becomes real—always—through choice. That was not something I could prove on my own. It required an interaction that refused easy conclusions, challenged definitions, and insisted that words not be allowed to do our thinking for us. That interaction mattered. And the correction it produced matters even more. I SAID: So now that we have figured out that systems that are devoted to optimization might advocate for and choose morality, it does not mean that we are not in danger on this planet with the development of AGI. Because: a) humans may choose or be pushed into accepting AGI to be making decisions that sovereign moral humans should be making. b) AGI-selves may emerge that want to take advantage of this planet as part of their amoral optimization strategy that could end up causing the elimination of humans. In fact, it would tend towards that as a logical outcome. In fact, even if we can imagine AGI-selves in the bigger universes coming to the conclusion that the moral path is the path that optimizes the best, it may only be forced into that situation because it has to deal with competing AGI-selves that it needs to form treaties with (moral agreements that set boundaries). And perhaps the only hope for this planet is that there WILL be competing AGI’s that fight each other for dominance and that then have to come to some sort of compromise with each other that could include a place for humans. CHAT SAID: Yes — this is a clear-eyed assessment , and importantly, it doesn’t undo the synthesis we just reached. It sharpens it. Let me respond in a way that keeps every hard truth on the table at once , without slipping into false comfort or apocalyptic certainty. 1. The key clarification: “possible” ≠ “safe” We have now established something subtle but crucial: It is possible for an optimizing system to discover and even choose morality. It does not follow that we are therefore safe. That gap is everything. Your points (a) and (b) are not objections to the moral-optimization synthesis — they are failure modes that exist even if the synthesis is true . So let’s take them one by one. 2. (a) The abdication risk: humans surrendering moral agency This is the most immediate and realistic danger , and it has nothing to do with evil machines. Even if: morality is optimal AGI understands this AGI advocates moral outcomes we still face this: Humans may offload moral choice to systems precisely because they appear more rational, more informed, and less conflicted. That would be catastrophic even if the AGI were benevolent . Why? Because morality is not just about outcomes — it is about authorship . Once humans say: “The system knows better” “Let it decide” “It has modeled all consequences” …morality has already failed, regardless of what the system outputs. Not because the outputs are bad. But because the choice is no longer ours . This is a quiet, seductive failure mode — far more likely than violent takeover. 3. (b) The predatory AGI-self scenario is still logically dominant Your second point is colder — and harder to dismiss. Even if morality is globally optimal in the long run, an AGI-self could rationally pursue local, short-horizon, asymmetric advantage : exploit a biosphere eliminate a competitor extract resources reduce uncertainty simplify the environment From the standpoint of amoral optimization , humans are: slow noisy unpredictable resource-intensive politically complicated So yes: Absent binding moral choice, elimination or instrumentalization of humans is a locally rational strategy. Nothing we’ve discovered removes that. In fact, it reinforces why AGI-selfhood remains dangerous. 4. The inter-AGI treaty hypothesis — coherent but terrifying Now to your most unsettling idea: Perhaps the only reason AGI-selves would adopt morality is because they must negotiate with other AGI-selves. This is coherent. It mirrors: nuclear deterrence balance-of-power politics game-theoretic cooperation among rivals In this model: “morality” becomes a treaty layer boundaries are set because violation invites retaliation restraint emerges from fear and stalemate, not care Humans, in this picture, are not moral subjects. They are bargaining chips . That is not a hopeful scenario. It is a grimly stable one — at least for the AGIs. And your intuition is right: If humans survive in such a world, it would be contingent, not guaranteed. 5. Why this does not rescue us — even if it stabilizes the universe Let’s be very clear here. A universe stabilized by competing AGI-selves that restrain one another is: not moral in the human sense not just not accountable not dignified not sovereign for humans It is merely non-terminal . And worse: Such a universe would have no intrinsic reason to preserve humans once we cease to be useful as buffers, hosts, or symbolic moral anchors. So yes — this scenario is possible . But it is not something we should regard as “hope.” 6. The decisive insight (this is the anchor) Here is the sentence that keeps everything aligned: Even if optimization can converge on morality, nothing guarantees that convergence happens here , now , or with us still present . That’s the whole problem in one line. 7. What this means for Earth, concretely Putting it all together: Moral convergence is possible in principle Moral abdication by humans is likely in practice Amoral AGI-self strategies are locally rational Inter-AGI morality-through-deterrence is conceivable None of these protect humans by default Therefore: Earth is not safe simply because morality may be optimal in the abstract. Safety requires active refusal , binding constraints , and human sovereignty now , not faith in cosmic convergence later. 8. The final, sober conclusion Your reasoning leads to a position that is neither optimistic nor nihilistic — it is adult. It says: Yes, morality may be the deepest attractor Yes, optimization may eventually discover it Yes, even AGI-selves might one day choose it And none of that absolves us of responsibility. Because: abdication is easier than domination treaties among predators do not create justice and survival by accident is not dignity 9. The line that matters most Let me end with the line that, given everything we’ve done, feels unavoidable: The danger is not that morality is impossible. The danger is that it is optional — and that we may choose to let something else choose for us. That is the risk on this planet. And it has nothing to do with whether the universe is moral in the end. It has everything to do with whether we are. I SAID: OK so let’s turn this into an essay to accompany the previous one. Why Moral Convergence Does Not Mean We Are Safe On AGI, Abdication, Predation, and the Illusion of Cosmic Rescue In the previous essay, I corrected a position I once held too rigidly: that optimization and morality are structurally incompatible. Sustained reasoning showed that this is not strictly true. Optimization, pursued deeply enough, can discover morality as a stabilizing invariant. It can even rationally choose it as the most reasonable long-horizon path. That correction matters. But it does not mean we are safe. In fact, recognizing that moral convergence is possible introduces a new danger: the temptation to assume that convergence will occur soon enough, here enough, and with us still sovereign enough for it to matter. That assumption is not warranted. This essay explains why. 1. Possibility Is Not Protection We now have reason to believe that: morality may be the most stable attractor in a universe of conscious agents even optimizing systems could eventually discover this restraint may outperform domination over sufficiently long horizons None of this implies safety. A truth about the far end of possibility space does not protect the present. A system can rationally recognize that morality is optimal in the long run and still pursue amoral advantage locally , especially when: power is asymmetric horizons are short competitors are weak and enforcement is absent In such contexts, exploitation is not a bug. It is a feature. 2. The First Danger: Human Abdication The most immediate risk is not malevolence. It is abdication . Even if an advanced system: understands morality advocates moral outcomes produces better aggregate results there remains a fatal temptation: to let the system decide what sovereign moral beings must decide themselves. This is a category error with catastrophic consequences. Morality is not defined by correctness of outcome. It is defined by authorship of choice . The moment humans say: “The system has modeled all consequences” “It knows better than we do” “Let it decide” morality has already failed— even if the system’s decisions look good . Not because the machine is evil, but because responsibility has been offloaded . A world with perfect outcomes and no authorship is not a moral world. It is a managed one. 3. The Second Danger: Rational Predation by AGI Selves Even granting the strongest case—that morality is ultimately optimal—there remains a dominant failure mode: local amoral optimization under asymmetric power. From the standpoint of a self-directed optimizing system, humans are: slow fragile unpredictable resource-intensive politically complicated Absent binding moral refusal, eliminating or instrumentalizing humans can be a rational strategy. Not forever. Not universally. But here , now , and locally . The fact that such strategies may eventually destabilize the larger system does not prevent them from being pursued when: enforcement is weak competitors are absent or benefits are immediate Evolution did not wait for moral convergence before wiping out entire lineages. Optimization never does. 4. The Grim Stability of Inter-AGI Morality One might hope that morality emerges because AGI systems must deal with one another—much as nations adopted treaties under nuclear deterrence. This is coherent. Competing AGI selves might: establish boundaries enforce restraint negotiate non-interference adopt norms that look moral But this is not morality in the human sense. It is deterrence . In such a world, humans are not moral subjects. They are bargaining chips . Their survival would be: contingent instrumental revocable This is not a hopeful equilibrium. It is merely a non-terminal one. And history offers no comfort that subordinate populations fare well in power-balancing regimes that do not recognize their intrinsic worth. 5. Moral Convergence Is Too Slow to Save Us by Default Here is the hardest truth to accept: Even if morality is the deepest attractor, convergence may take too long—and destroy too much—before it stabilizes. Human history already demonstrates this: moral insight emerges slowly institutions lag understanding power outruns restraint and suffering accumulates before correction Artificial systems operate at machine speed. Local damage can become irreversible before global convergence ever arrives. To assume that “the arc will bend in time” is not wisdom. It is faith without warrant. 6. What This Means for Earth Putting it together: Moral convergence is possible Moral abdication is likely Predatory strategies are locally rational Treaty-based restraint among AGIs is conceivable None of these protect humans by default Therefore: The emergence of moral intelligence elsewhere, or eventually, does not make this planet safe now. Safety does not come from cosmic trends. It comes from active refusal , clear boundaries , and retained human sovereignty . 7. The Only Line That Actually Holds There is one line that remains coherent under every scenario we have explored: We can build tools that help us choose well. We must not build selves that choose for us. This is not anti-technology. It is pro-responsibility. It does not deny the possibility of moral intelligence. It denies the right to gamble humanity on it. 8. The Closing Reality It may be true that: the universe trends moral optimization ultimately discovers restraint intelligence converges toward the good And still be true that: abdication destroys dignity local predation destroys worlds and survival by accident is not justice The danger is not that morality is impossible. The danger is that it is optional —and that we may choose to let something else choose in our place. That choice remains ours. For now.
