Part IIIa: Monsanto Was Not an Outlier — It Was a Node harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Part IIIa: Monsanto Was Not an Outlier — It Was a Node How agricultural biotechnology completed the loop: licensing webs, regulatory capture and the export of visibility when risk becomes politically radioactive harry blazer Jan 05, 2026 2 Share Monsanto is commonly treated as an aberration — a uniquely reckless corporation whose behavior triggered public backlash and eventual absorption. That framing is comforting. It is also wrong. Monsanto was not an anomaly in the system. It was a node — a highly visible concentration point within a much larger, more resilient network. When the node became unstable, the network rerouted. Nodes vs. Systems: Why Visibility Is the Real Liability In mature power systems, visibility is risk . A company becomes dangerous to itself when: its brand is widely recognized its products are emotionally charged its harms are legible to non-experts its name becomes shorthand for systemic critique Monsanto crossed all four thresholds: Glyphosate GMOs Seed monopolization Farmer litigation narratives At that point, Monsanto was no longer just a producer. It had become a symbolic focal point . And symbols that threaten the system are expendable. The Licensing Web: Control Without Ownership The real power of agricultural biotechnology never resided primarily in product sales. It resided in: intellectual property trait licensing regulatory pathway ownership standards-setting Monsanto’s GMO traits were: licensed across seed companies embedded in multiple brands protected by patent law enforced through contract, not policing This meant something crucial: Monsanto did not need to dominate the market to control it. Once traits become standard, the ecosystem itself enforces compliance . This is the same logic we saw with: software formats financial clearing systems chemical feedstocks Ownership becomes optional. Dependence becomes structural. Regulatory Capture Without Conspiracy Agricultural biotechnology illustrates regulatory capture in its most normalized form. Not overt bribery. Not overt corruption. But epistemic dependence . Agencies such as the Environmental Protection Agency and US Department of Agriculture increasingly relied on: industry-funded studies proprietary data confidential business information revolving-door expertise This creates a quiet asymmetry: Regulators must trust what they cannot independently replicate Industry controls the evidence environment Oversight becomes procedural rather than substantive At that point, regulation no longer constrains innovation that damages. It stabilizes it . And regulators declaring GMO food equivalent to non-GMO food while respecting the technology as proprietary IP, was a critical enabler on several fronts. When Risk Becomes Politically Radioactive The turning point for Monsanto was not scientific. It was political . As litigation and judgments mounted and public trust collapsed, Monsanto’s problem was no longer whether glyphosate was defensible. It was that glyphosate had become discussable . That is the danger state. When scrutiny becomes accessible to laypeople, the system does not double down. It restructures . The Export of Visibility: Monsanto → Bayer Monsanto’s acquisition by Bayer is often described as a corporate rescue or consolidation. Structurally, it was something else: Visibility export. The Monsanto name disappeared The product lines persisted The chemistry endured The regulatory pathways remained intact The licensing logic survived The node was dissolved. The network remained. This mirrors what we saw with: DuPont → Chemours / Corteva Banks → custodians Intelligence → contractors Brand death, capability survival. DuPont, Corteva and the Parallel Track In parallel, DuPont followed a structurally identical path. Through mergers and separations, its agricultural arm emerged as Corteva , carrying: seed genetics chemical expertise regulatory familiarity licensing relationships What matters is not rivalry between Monsanto and DuPont. What matters is convergence . Different nodes. Same architecture. Agriculture as the Perfect Control Domain Agriculture is uniquely powerful because it sits at the intersection of: biology survival scale time dependency Unlike software: farmers cannot “opt out” cycles are seasonal switching costs are existential contamination is non-local Once an agricultural technology becomes standard: refusal becomes impractical dissent becomes marginal alternatives disappear quietly This is control without coercion . Structural Parallel to Everything Else By now, the repetition should feel familiar: DomainPatternNuclearImmunity via statutePharmaImmunity via administrationITImmunity via essentialityPFASImmunity via permanenceAgricultureImmunity via dependency Monsanto did not invent this. It demonstrated it vividly . Why Monsanto Had to Disappear The system did not punish Monsanto. It retired it . Once a node attracts too much attention: it threatens the invisibility of the structure it becomes a rallying point it destabilizes trust The solution is not reform. The solution is absorption and erasure . Monsanto was not removed because the system rejected its practices. It was removed because it revealed them too clearly and in turn threatened the system. This pattern tells us something critical about future controversies: When a company is loudly demonized When its name disappears but its products remain When outrage is resolved without structural change You are not witnessing accountability. You are witnessing node replacement . The structure is still intact.
