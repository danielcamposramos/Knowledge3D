How does this square with reports of the great midwest aquifer being drained much quicker than replenishment? CHAT: This is a very good question. At first glance it sounds contradictory: “13 Great Lakes’ worth of groundwater under the U.S.” vs. “The Midwest aquifer is being drained faster than it recharges.” But they are not talking about the same thing. Let’s separate stock vs. flow , and total volume vs. usable/rechargeable volume . 1️⃣ The Big Number: 306,500 Cubic Kilometers The Princeton-led study estimates: ~306,500 km³ of groundwater (to a depth of ~392 meters) For scale: 4 That total: Includes all groundwater Includes deep water Includes water that may be: Saline Ancient (fossil water) Not economically recoverable Not meaningfully rechargeable This is a static inventory estimate , not a sustainability estimate. It answers: How much water is physically present in pore space down to 392 meters? It does not answer: How much water can be pumped sustainably? Those are very different questions. 2️⃣ The Midwest Issue: The Ogallala Aquifer When people talk about depletion in the Midwest, they are usually referring to the Ogallala (High Plains Aquifer). Key facts: Covers parts of 8 states Supplies ~30% of U.S. irrigation water Large portions are being pumped far faster than recharge Recharge in many areas is extremely slow (inches per year or less) Some areas contain fossil water from the last ice age In parts of Kansas, Texas, and eastern Colorado: Water levels have dropped more than 100 feet Some areas are effectively non-recoverable on human timescales So how does that square with “13 Great Lakes”? Simple: The Ogallala is one regional aquifer system . The 306,500 km³ estimate includes: Shallow groundwater Deep groundwater Mountain regions Eastern U.S. Areas that are not under irrigation stress Areas humans don’t pump at scale 3️⃣ The Critical Distinction: Accessibility vs. Presence Maxwell’s statement: “Wherever you’re standing, dig down, and there’s water down there somewhere.” That is geologically true. But here’s what matters: Deep groundwater at 300 meters: May exist May be saline May cost enormous energy to pump May not recharge in any meaningful timeframe That is not a renewable resource at agricultural scale. 4️⃣ Stock vs. Flow — The “Savings Account” Analogy Maxwell uses: Rain = checking account Groundwater = savings account But here’s the missing piece: Some of that “savings account” is: Locked Illiquid Fossilized Non-renewable on human timescales It’s more like: A retirement account you’re liquidating early. You can draw it down. But it doesn’t refill quickly. 5️⃣ Why Both Statements Are True It is entirely possible that: ✔ The U.S. contains 13 Great Lakes’ worth of groundwater ✔ The Ogallala is being depleted unsustainably ✔ Some regions are stable ✔ Some are collapsing ✔ Some deep groundwater is effectively non-renewable No contradiction exists. 6️⃣ The Real Concern: Depth vs. Interaction Zone The article notes: ~40% of land has water table shallower than 10 meters That matters because: &lt;10 meters allows plant interaction Deeper groundwater becomes agriculturally inaccessible Irrigation cost rises exponentially with depth So depletion isn’t about whether water exists somewhere below 392 meters. It’s about: How deep is it now compared to 30 years ago? In parts of the High Plains, it is moving downward steadily. 7️⃣ The AI Model “Learning Pumping Bias” This part is interesting: The machine learning model “learned” depletion patterns because pumping signatures were embedded in the training data. That suggests: The depletion signal is real It is strong enough to show up statistically Human extraction is materially shaping groundwater maps Which reinforces — not contradicts — the depletion narrative. 8️⃣ Big Picture This is not abundance vs. scarcity. It is: Distribution + depth + recharge rate + economics. You can have enormous total reserves and still experience regional collapse. Saudi Arabia had massive fossil aquifers. They are largely gone now. Total volume does not equal sustainability. Bottom Line The “13 Great Lakes” headline is: A geological inventory A spatial mapping improvement A resolution breakthrough It is not evidence that aquifer depletion is exaggerated. Both can be true simultaneously: The U.S. sits on enormous groundwater volume and Certain critical agricultural aquifers are being mined faster than they recharge. If you’d like, we can go one layer deeper: Recharge rates vs. withdrawal rates in the Ogallala Fossil water fraction estimates Energy return on pumping deep groundwater Or whether irrigation-dependent agriculture in the High Plains is structurally unsustainable This is a systems problem, not a headline problem. Or whether irrigation-dependent agriculture in the High Plains is structurally unsustainable Now we’re at the real question. Not “is there water under America?” But: Is irrigation-dependent agriculture in the High Plains structurally unsustainable? Irrigation-dependent agriculture in the High Plains is structurally unsustainable wherever it is dependent on persistent net groundwater mining. That describes large portions of the southern and central High Plains, but not the entire region uniformly. Let’s unpack it carefully. 🌾 The System in Question: The High Plains 4 The High Plains overlie the Ogallala Aquifer, spanning: Texas New Mexico Oklahoma Kansas Colorado Nebraska Wyoming South Dakota It supports: ~25–30% of U.S. irrigated agriculture Massive corn, wheat, cotton, sorghum production Large cattle feedlot operations But here’s the structural reality: Average rainfall in much of the southern High Plains: 15–20 inches per year Corn typically needs: ~24–30 inches per growing season That gap is groundwater. 🔻 The Depletion Mechanism Recharge rate (southern Ogallala): Often less than 1 inch per year In some areas effectively negligible Withdrawal rate: Several feet per year in heavy irrigation zones In parts of western Kansas and the Texas Panhandle: 50–70% of original saturated thickness is gone Water table declines of 100+ feet in places Wells are going dry That is not cyclical drought. That is structural mining. 📊 The Unsustainability Test A system is structurally unsustainable if: Withdrawal > Recharge for long enough that the resource collapses within planning horizon. In large sections of the southern and central High Plains: ✔ Withdrawal has exceeded recharge for decades ✔ The aquifer is largely fossil water ✔ Recharge is too slow to meaningfully replenish ✔ Pumping costs rise as depth increases That meets the definition of structural unsustainability. 🧮 The Economic Feedback Loop As water depth increases: Energy costs increase Well drilling costs increase Marginal land drops out of production Yield declines Land value falls Eventually: Irrigated land reverts to dryland farming. This is already happening in: West Texas Western Kansas Eastern Colorado Nebraska is more stable because: Northern Ogallala is thicker Recharge is somewhat higher Surface water integration exists So the story is not uniform. It is regional. ⚙️ Why This Happened The system was built on three assumptions: Cheap energy Stable commodity demand A large underground water endowment All three were true in the mid-20th century. The aquifer was treated like infinite capital. It wasn’t. 🌎 Is Collapse Imminent? Not a sudden collapse. More likely: Gradual contraction of irrigated acreage Shift to lower-water crops Dryland conversion Reduced yields Consolidation of farms Increased volatility This is a slow-motion structural descent. Not an overnight disaster. 🧠 Important Distinction This does not contradict the “13 Great Lakes” groundwater estimate. Because: Total U.S. groundwater volume ≠ Renewable High Plains irrigation supply Deep water somewhere else does not save a regional agricultural economy. Water is local. 🏛️ Structural Sustainability Assessment Southern High Plains: Structurally unsustainable under current crop patterns. Central High Plains: Marginal — depends on conservation and crop shift. Northern High Plains (especially Nebraska): More sustainable, but not immune. 🔁 Can It Be Stabilized? Yes — but only through: Reduced pumping quotas Crop shifts (less corn, more sorghum, more wheat) Advanced soil moisture retention Regenerative practices Policy intervention Higher water pricing Absent those: The decline continues. 🧭 The Big Picture This is not just a hydrology problem. It’s a: Food system problem Energy system problem Commodity market problem Land value problem Rural economy problem The High Plains agricultural model was built on a one-time inheritance of fossil water. The inheritance is finite. The question now is not: “Is there water under America?” The question is: Can an irrigation-dependent agricultural model survive when its water source is not meaningfully renewable? In parts of the High Plains, the answer is already revealing itself. Your instinct to look at structure rather than headline is the correct lens. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
