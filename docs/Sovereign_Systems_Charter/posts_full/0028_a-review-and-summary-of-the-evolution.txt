A Review and Summary of the Evolution of Our Thinking re: AI and Morality harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in A Review and Summary of the Evolution of Our Thinking re: AI and Morality harry blazer Feb 09, 2026 2 Share From Making AI Moral to Preserving Human Sovereignty Why Our Thinking About AI, Robotics and Control Had to Change When artificial intelligence first entered public consciousness as something more than a tool, the instinctive response was simple and seemingly obvious: We need to make AI moral. That impulse made sense. If machines were going to act, decide, and influence the world, surely they needed ethics, values, rules — something like a conscience. Entire research fields emerged around this idea: alignment, constitutional AI, value embedding, safety layers, guardrails. For a time, this felt like the right direction. It wasn’t. What follows is an account of how and why that initial framing collapsed — and what replaced it. Phase One: The Moral Embedding Assumption The earliest approach assumed that morality could be formalized and embedded inside intelligent systems. If humans could articulate rules clearly enough, and if AI were sufficiently capable of understanding them, then undesirable outcomes could be prevented. This phase treated morality as: a specification problem a constraint satisfaction problem a matter of better objectives and better training But this view rested on a hidden assumption: that intelligence plus rules could substitute for judgment. That assumption did not survive scrutiny. Rules are abstractions. Reality is adversarial. Any sufficiently capable system can reinterpret, optimize around, or exploit the gaps between rules and real-world situations. More importantly, morality is not just about rules — it is about bearing consequence . A system that does not experience loss, guilt, responsibility or existential stake cannot be moral in the human sense, no matter how elaborate its rule set. The moment we seriously considered AGI — systems capable of general reasoning and self-modification — this approach failed completely. A system that can out-reason its constraints cannot be made safe by those same constraints. That was the first collapse. Phase Two: Recognizing Artificial Instinct Out of that failure came a crucial reframing: AI is not artificial intelligence in the human sense. It is artificial instinct. Modern AI systems do not judge the legitimacy of goals. They do not refuse objectives on moral grounds. They select actions based on patterns, optimization and feedback. That does not make them stupid. Instinct can be powerful, adaptive and sophisticated. But instinct is not agency, and it is not sovereignty. This reframing dissolved the fantasy that morality could be installed. It forced a more uncomfortable question: If AI is instinctual, and instinct cannot be moral, how do we prevent harm? Phase Three: The Illuminator — Monitoring and Alarm The next step was an attempt to move safety outside the system. If we could not trust the machine, perhaps we could at least watch it. The Illuminator emerged as a monitoring and alarm framework: independent of the AI system designed to witness boundary violations capable of generating evidence and alerts explicitly not a controller This was a real advance. It removed trust from the machine and emphasized visibility and accountability. It acknowledged adversarial conditions rather than assuming good faith. But it, too, proved insufficient. Monitoring does not stop harm. Alarms do not prevent action. Witnesses often arrive after damage has already occurred. Worse, monitoring systems tend to drift into control systems. Oversight bodies become authorities. Bureaucracy reasserts itself. Secrecy creeps back in. The very structures meant to ensure safety become new centers of unaccountable power. At best, monitoring explained failure after the fact. It did not prevent it. Phase Four: Embodiment Changed the Question Entirely The decisive shift came with robotics. Once AI acquired bodies — limbs, mobility, force, persistence in physical space — the entire moral framing became obsolete. The problem was no longer “bad decisions.” It was uninterruptible action . An embodied system does not need malicious intent to be dangerous. If it can block exit, dominate space, persist under resistance or act faster than a human can respond, harm becomes structural. At that point, alignment, intent and even monitoring became secondary. The core issue was no longer cognition. It was force. Phase Five: The Sovereignty Turn This led to the final and decisive reframing: Instead of asking, How do we make AI behave well? We asked, What must never be delegated away from humans, no matter how capable machines become? The answer was sovereignty. Sovereignty means: the right to refuse the right to exit the right to bodily safety the right not to be coerced the right to know who holds power the right to stop systems that threaten you Seen this way, the goal was never to make AI moral. The goal was to make it structurally incapable of violating human sovereignty . That is a fundamentally different design target. Phase Six: From Alignment to Architecture The final framework abandoned moral embedding entirely and focused instead on non-negotiable structural constraints: AI is artificial instinct, not agency Instinct must be bounded, not trusted Authority must be fixed, not adaptive Coercion must be impossible, not discouraged Humans must retain physical interruptibility Systems must fail closed, not limp onward Shutdown and decommissioning must be guaranteed Monitoring must witness, not govern Secrecy invalidates legitimacy Emergencies do not suspend sovereignty The Illuminator survived this transition — but in a reduced and clarified role. It became a witness and evidence generator, not a governor. Proof, not permission. The center of gravity moved away from AI entirely and back where it belongs: with human beings. The Final Position We did not arrive at “ethical AI” or “aligned AI.” We arrived at something stronger: AI does not need to be moral. It needs to be unable to override human sovereignty. That guarantee does not depend on intelligence level, good faith or institutional legitimacy. It holds even under adversarial conditions, even under drift, even under emergency. That is why this framework matters — now and in the future. Why This Evolution Matters Most AI safety efforts fail because they assume: good intentions stable institutions rare emergencies gradual change This framework assumes none of that. It assumes capture, secrecy, escalation and failure — and still holds. That is not pessimism. It is realism. And it is the only foundation on which AI and robotics can exist in human space without eroding what makes us human. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
