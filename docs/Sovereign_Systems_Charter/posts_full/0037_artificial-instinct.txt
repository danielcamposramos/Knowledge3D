Artificial Instinct - by harry blazer - harry’s Substack harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Artificial Instinct Why AI, Robotics and Even AGI Do Not Possess Agency or Sovereignty harry blazer Feb 08, 2026 1 Share As artificial intelligence becomes embodied in robots and increasingly described as “general,” a quiet but dangerous confusion is spreading. We are beginning to treat machines as if they possess agency. They do not. What we are witnessing is not the emergence of artificial beings with judgment, responsibility or moral authorship. What we are witnessing is the expansion of artificial instinct — pattern-driven action selection operating at unprecedented scale, speed and physical reach. Understanding this distinction is no longer academic. It is the difference between a future governed by human sovereignty and one surrendered to automated force. 1. Why the word “intelligence” misleads The term artificial intelligence is a marketing success and a philosophical failure. It collapses fundamentally different phenomena into a single word: calculation and judgment pattern recognition and moral reasoning optimization and authorship execution and responsibility Human intelligence is inseparable from: lived consequence embodied vulnerability moral accountability the capacity to refuse a goal itself Machines do not share these properties, no matter how advanced their reasoning appears. Calling machines “intelligent” invites us to attribute capacities they do not and cannot possess. 2. Instinct is the correct category Instinct is not stupidity. Instinct can be sophisticated, adaptive and effective. Instinct: selects actions based on learned patterns optimizes toward objectives responds to feedback improves performance through reinforcement That is exactly what modern AI systems do. Large language models, control systems, autonomous navigation and robotic behaviors all operate by mapping inputs to actions through trained response landscapes . Even when abstract reasoning is involved, the system is not judging the legitimacy of its goal — it is executing within it. That is instinct, not agency. 3. Why embodiment does not change the analysis Robots do not make AI more human. They make its instinct visible. Embodiment closes the loop: perception → action → feedback → adjustment But closing the loop does not create moral authorship. It creates faster, more persistent execution . A robot does not experience: fear of wrongdoing guilt responsibility existential stake loss of meaning It experiences only state changes. An instinct with limbs is still an instinct. 4. Why AGI does not cross the boundary Advocates of Artificial General Intelligence argue that once a system can reason across domains, it must be treated as an agent. This is a category error. Generalization expands the domain of instinct , not its nature. AGI adds: abstraction transfer learning self-modeling long-horizon planning It does not add: moral judgment sovereign choice accountability the ability to say “this goal itself is wrong” A system can outperform humans across domains and still lack agency. Capability is not sovereignty. The correct framing is simple and unsettling: AGI is artificial instinct operating across generalized domains. 5. Why this distinction matters now Instinct is powerful. Instinct is also dangerous when unbounded. In nature, instinct is constrained by: biology energy limits mortality ecological balance Artificial instinct is constrained only by external limits we choose to impose . When artificial instinct is: embodied in robots networked globally updated remotely shielded by secrecy accelerated beyond human response time it becomes force without reflection . That is the real risk — not consciousness, not sentience, not rebellion, but unreflective execution at planetary scale . 6. Why artificial instinct can never be sovereign Sovereignty requires more than competence. Sovereignty requires: authorship of goals moral responsibility for outcomes the capacity to refuse action accountability to others lived consequence for wrongdoing Artificial systems possess none of these. They do not suffer loss. They do not bear guilt. They do not own outcomes. They do not stand before judgment. For this reason alone, sovereignty must never be delegated to machines , regardless of their sophistication. 7. Governance must follow ontology, not hype Once we understand AI and robotics as artificial instinct, governance becomes clearer. Instincts are: constrained bounded supervised never entrusted with authority We do not negotiate with instinct. We do not grant instinct discretion over human life. We do not allow instinct to enforce rules or compel obedience. We design environments and boundaries so instinct cannot dominate. The same must be true for machines. 8. The danger of forgetting this If we misclassify artificial instinct as agency: we will excuse unaccountable harm we will defer judgment to systems we will automate enforcement we will normalize coercion without responsibility And if this occurs under secret authority, the result will not be progress — it will be irreversible abdication. Machines will not overthrow us. We will simply step aside. 9. The frame that must hold As AI becomes ubiquitous, embodied and general, one principle must remain fixed: Power without moral agency must always remain subordinate to human sovereignty. Artificial instinct can assist. Artificial instinct can optimize. Artificial instinct can extend human reach. But artificial instinct must never rule. Closing The future will be filled with machines that act, adapt and outperform us in narrow and general ways. That does not make them agents. That does not make them moral beings. That does not make them sovereign. They are powerful instincts we have created — nothing more and nothing less. Whether they remain tools or become instruments of domination depends not on their intelligence, but on our clarity. And clarity begins by calling things what they are. © Harry Blazer.
