Capital expenditures are long-term investments to acquire, upgrade or extend the useful life of its fixed assets (the tools, infrastructure, capabilities required to operate and grow) harry blazer Nov 26, 2025 Share Meta’s 2025 capital expenditures (CAPEX) forecast is US$70 billion to US$72 billion . Reuters+2Data Center Dynamics+2 The company signaled that 2026 CAPEX will be “notably larger” than 2025—but did not provide a firm number. Data Center Dynamics+2Sherwood News+2 Analysts estimate Meta’s 2026 CAPEX could approach ~US$97 billion . AP News Caveats The “~US$97 billion” figure is analyst-derived , not officially confirmed by Meta. Meta has clarified that part of infrastructure spending may be structured off-balance or via joint ventures (which affect how CAPEX is recorded) especially for large data center builds. Data Center Dynamics CAPEX includes a broad range of infrastructure: data-centers, servers, networking, cooling/power systems, third-party cloud contracts. The AI emphasis is strong, but the spending supports core business too. Because the figure for 2026 is “notably larger” and not pinned, any estimates should be treated as preliminary. MacroTrends+1 So they are spending 40%+ of SALES on CAPEX, which means their margins are astronomical compared to legacy industrial/retail/service businesses. Their financial performance and investment strategies are staggering when you compare it to any legacy-economy business. 1. Meta’s CAPEX ≈ 43% of Revenue Revenue: ~$165B CAPEX 2025: $70–72B That’s ~42–44% of total sales . For comparison, traditional sectors: Retail (Walmart, Target): 2–4% of sales Grocery: 1–2% Telecom: 12–18% Utilities / Energy: 15–25% Automotive: 5–7% Airlines: 10–12% Manufacturing: 5–8% Nobody in the legacy industrial world comes anywhere close to 40% of revenue being plowed back into physical/infrastructure CAPEX. Meta is basically operating like: A sovereign state building a global-scale digital energy grid. 2. WHY Their Margins Allow This Meta’s gross margin is around 80% . Operating margin sits around 40–45% . So it is doing something that Amazon was doing - basically investing almost all free cash flow into infrastructure. You do this when the intention is to dominate your business sector. Compare CAPEX as % of sales in the following industries: Grocery: 1–3% Restaurants: 3–6% Big box retail: 2–5% Banks: 20–30%v (mostly in digital technologies at this point I would guess) Oil & gas: 5–15% Telcos: 10–15% Meta is basically printing cash at levels legacy industries never experience. Meta can spend $70B/year because: It has almost no cost of goods No inventory No physical distribution constraints No labor-intensive workflows No spoilage, shrink, returns No transportation No gross margin erosion through commodities No capital tied up in stores or warehouses The product is attention , and attention has nearly infinite gross margin once infrastructure is built. 3. Meta’s CAPEX is Not “Normal CAPEX” — It’s Empire-Building Meta is not buying forklifts, trucks or stores. Its CAPEX is: massive data centers massive GPU clusters massive compute grids custom silicon network topology private fiber redundant hyperscale cooling systems AI training facilities digital power plants resource-mapped global compute farms This is nation-state scale investment , not corporate scale. They’re building infrastructure comparable to: the interstate highway system the electrical grid telecom backbone military intelligence compute clusters And they’re doing it privately , with no oversight, because digital infrastructure is unregulated compared to energy and transportation. 4. Consider: Legacy industrial companies invest CAPEX so they can produce things . Meta invests CAPEX so it can produce control . And control has: no unit cost infinite scalability recursive feedback loops a flywheel effect no ceiling no market boundaries almost no competition enormous lock-in In other words, AI infrastructure is the new utility , but controlled by private entities rather than states. 5. What This Signals Systemically Spending almost ½ of revenue on CAPEX indicates: Meta believes compute is the decisive battleground of the next decade AI capability curves justify massive upfront spending The return is exponential rather than linear They intend to dominate training infrastructure, not just applications They foresee a world where compute = dominance CAPEX is the moat — not algorithms The goal is to build a digital monopoly that no startup could ever catch This is the same logic Amazon used by building AWS, but now on steroids. 6. Conclusion Meta’s margins are so astronomical that it is playing an entirely different economic game than physical-world businesses. Legacy businesses operate on: thin margins real-world constraints labor intensity physical inventory regulation geography Meta operates on: software economics attention extraction digital scaling no geographic constraint practically infinite growth curves AI as a force multiplier margins that would make a 20th century CEO faint This is why companies like Meta, Google, Microsoft, and Amazon can spend tens of billions annually while still appearing financially invincible. The numbers are insane — and they only really make sense if you think of these firms as quasi-sovereign infrastructure states , not “companies.” 1. Meta vs Google vs Microsoft vs Amazon — CAPEX vs Sales So yes, Meta is spending on the order of 40+% of last year’s sales on CAPEX , and the others aren’t that far behind on a sheer-dollar basis. 2. CAPEX as % of Revenue — Hyperscalers vs “Normal” Industries To see how wild this is, compare to sectors that we think of as “capital intensive”: U.S. regulated electric & gas utilities : 2024 capex ≈ $178.2B on revenue ≈ $403.5B → ~44% of revenue EEI That’s comparable to Meta’s CAPEX intensity. Most other sectors are dramatically lower: Tech sector broadly: capex-to-revenue ~5–10% historically Many consumer / service sectors (retail, restaurants, services) are often in the low single digits of revenue Even “heavy” sectors (manufacturing, transport) typically live in the high single to low double digits , not 25–40% every year One recent benchmark of capex-to-revenue by sector shows most U.S. sectors in single-digit or low-teens territory, with Technology around ~7% on average and Communication Services even lower. Strategic Meaning: AI Infrastructure as a Sovereign Asset a. CAPEX is buying compute + energy + geography When these players say “CAPEX,” they’re really buying: Land, power contracts and transmission Data centers, cooling, substations GPUs / TPUs / custom accelerators Fiber routes and network backbones Long-term energy and water access So in practice they are securing: Physical territory (land and siting) Energy throughput (GW of power, long-term contracts) Information gravity (data + models + traffic) Talent gravity (AI labs anchored to that infra) That is exactly what states used to do with: ports rail highways power grids telecom networks Now it’s being done by corporations , at global scale, with minimal external oversight. b. The real moat is not the model, it’s the information/power grid Models can be copied, open-sourced or leapfrogged. What cannot be easily replicated: 400+ data centers in 70+ regions (Microsoft) Microsoft+1 Tens of gigawatts of secured power Hundreds of billions in sunk computation infrastructure This means: Capital → buys compute Compute → trains models, hosts agents Models/agents → capture users, enterprises, governments Usage → justifies more CAPEX More CAPEX → widens moat Classic flywheel, but now at civilizational scale . c. From “cloud provider” to “digital utility” They are converging toward the role of: Electric company for cognition Water company for data Railroad for AI agents Except: They are not regulated like utilities They can pick and choose customers globally They can weaponize pricing, access and latency They can embed policy in code (AUPs, filters, defaults) So AI infrastructure is effectively a sovereign asset without a sovereign — privately controlled but socially systemic. Thus, revenue sources reflect where the leverage lies.Essay: Digital Empire-Building and the New Sovereignty Crisis A Closing Essay: Digital Empire-Building and the New Sovereignty Crisis When a company spends seventy billion dollars in a year on metal boxes that think, it is no longer just “a business.” It is building a grid. Not a power grid, not a highway system, not a phone network. A cognition grid . Meta, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon — they are not simply scaling servers to build capacity. They are quietly assembling the infrastructure of perception itself : the data centers that will host our news, our conversations, our AI copilots, our synthetic teachers, our digital doctors, our simulated “selves.” The old empires fought for land, ports, rail, oil. The new empires fight for compute . Compute is not just faster arithmetic. Compute is: who gets to train the most powerful models who sets the defaults for what counts as “true” who can personalize reality at scale who can afford to run agents 24/7 over every stream of human activity CAPEX that once would have built roads and factories is now building machine minds and their temples . And unlike roads or power plants, these assets are almost entirely controlled by private actors with global reach and local accountability to no one. This is the new sovereignty crisis: States still control territory Corporations increasingly control cognition Humans sit in the crossfire, mistaking personalized interfaces for agency We are watching a quiet inversion of power. The nation-state still flies the flag, but the hyperscaler owns the infrastructure that shapes what the flag means to its citizens. The question is no longer “Who owns the means of production?” It is “Who owns the means of perception?” As long as AI infrastructure is concentrated in a handful of private empires, human sovereignty will exist at their discretion — revocable by ToS update (terms of service), policy change, power contract or silent model retrain.
