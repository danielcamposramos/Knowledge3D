DEVELOPING A MORAL CODE BY DEFINING EVIL - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in DEVELOPING A MORAL CODE BY DEFINING EVIL Sovereignty Requires Responsibility harry blazer Dec 16, 2025 1 Share We live in an age that is deeply confused about evil. Some reduce it to pathology. Others to bad incentives. Others to ignorance. Others to “systems.” And still others externalize it entirely — as something done to us, rather than something done by us. Each of these framings contains a partial truth. None of them is sufficient. Worse, many of them serve as moral anesthetics, allowing intelligent, well-intentioned people to participate in grave harm while sincerely believing they are doing good. What follows is an attempt to articulate a coherent, refutable and sovereignty-preserving framework for understanding evil, moral responsibility and resistance — one that does not collapse under crisis, does not outsource conscience to authority and does not allow “good intentions” to become a shield against accountability. I. A Working Definition of Evil Evil may be defined as an intentional act or system designed to replace, erode or foreclose the present or future sovereignty of another human being. This definition deliberately avoids equating evil with: suffering alone bad outcomes error ignorance tragedy Suffering can occur without evil. Mistakes can be made without evil. Harm can occur without evil. Evil enters when human sovereignty is intentionally targeted — when a person is treated not as a moral author of their own life, but as an object to be managed, optimized, coerced or sacrificed. This definition immediately clarifies several things. It explains why manipulation can be evil even when painless. Why coercion can be evil even when “benevolent.” Why systems can be evil without sadistic actors. And why evil often presents itself as reasonable, efficient and humanitarian. II. Sovereignty Is Developmental, Not Static Sovereignty is not identical at all stages of human life. A child is not yet a fully sovereign being. The moral responsibility of a parent or guardian is therefore not to maximize a child’s immediate autonomy, but to preserve and cultivate the child’s future sovereignty . Preventing a child from running into traffic is not a violation of sovereignty. It is an act in service of sovereignty not yet realized. This distinction matters, because it prevents the framework from collapsing into caricature. Not every restraint is evil. Not every limitation of choice is domination. The critical test is this: Does the act preserve, restore or enable a person’s capacity to become and remain a self-directing moral agent over time, or does it replace that agency with external control? III. Error, Harm and Derivative Evil Not all harm is evil. But harm imposes responsibility. A sovereign human being is obligated to understand that actions have consequences, including unintended consequences. That obligation increases with: time for deliberation access to information position of authority scale of impact There is a crucial moral inflection point that must not be blurred: Once credible evidence of harm emerges, persistence becomes a moral choice. At that point, original intent no longer absolves responsibility. Continuing to enforce, defend or rationalize a harmful policy after evidence appears constitutes derivative participation in evil , even if the harm was not initially intended. This is where many systems fail. Learning is deferred. Doubt is suppressed. Dissent is pathologized. And “we meant well” becomes a moral shield. Good faith behavior looks different. It involves: transparency of reasoning and motives openness to critique and refutation immediate course correction when harm appears admission of error refusal to persist for the sake of authority or image Where these behaviors disappear, moral credibility disappears with them. IV. Coercion Does Not Eliminate Responsibility There are circumstances in which individuals are coerced under threat — to their careers, their families, their safety or their lives — to participate in actions they know will cause harm to others. Coercion explains behavior. It does not erase moral responsibility. A society that accepts “I was just following orders” as a sufficient defense has already abandoned sovereignty. This does not mean all coerced individuals are equally culpable. Responsibility exists on a gradient. But it never vanishes. At minimum, sovereignty demands: refusal to become an instrument of harm withdrawal from participation where possible exposure of wrongdoing acceptance of personal cost rather than outsourcing cost onto innocents Sometimes that cost is severe. History is unambiguous on this point. Moral adulthood has never been safe. V. Force, Resistance and Moral Continuity A framework that categorically denies the legitimacy of self-defense is incoherent. Sovereignty that must submit to annihilation is not sovereignty at all. Force can be morally legitimate when it is directed toward stopping an active or imminent violation of sovereignty. But this truth carries an equally binding constraint: Force does not suspend moral responsibility. It amplifies it. Resistance does not grant a moral blank check. War does not nullify the obligation to preserve innocent life. Ends do not erase means. When resistance abandons moral continuity — when civilians are instrumentalized, when dehumanization becomes acceptable, when “victory” excuses anything — evil has not been defeated. It has replicated itself. This is why many revolutions fail. They satisfy the mandate to resist evil, while violating the equally binding mandate not to become evil in the process . VI. Moral Outrage Is Not the Enemy of Reason Moral clarity does not arise from cognition alone. Human moral intelligence is triune: mind, which seeks coherence heart, which refuses instrumentalization gut, which recognizes violation before it can be articulated Moral outrage is not a defect. It is energy. What matters is whether that energy is disciplined rather than unleashed indiscriminately. Systems that suppress moral outrage entirely do not become more rational. They become morally anesthetized. At the same time, outrage without discipline becomes destructive. Moral power emerges when emotion, reason and conscience are aligned , not when one is amputated. VII. Systems Matter, but Individuals Are Irreducible No system can replace conscience. Systems can: facilitate scrutiny require transparency slow down harm distribute information But systems cannot: assume guilt bear responsibility choose sacrifice act justly on behalf of another Every attempt to outsource moral responsibility to systems ends the same way: individuals are trained to obey rather than judge, and sovereignty collapses at its source. A social group does not minimize the moral responsibility of the individual. It magnifies it. Societies fail not because they lack intelligence, but because individuals are conditioned to surrender moral authorship. VIII. A Final Synthesis We can now state the framework plainly. Evil is the intentional destruction of human sovereignty. Resistance to evil is morally required. Resistance must remain sovereignty-preserving. Harm without learning becomes culpability. Coercion does not erase responsibility. Force does not suspend morality. Systems may guide, but individuals must judge. Movements may inspire, but individuals must act. The preservation of civilization depends not on better systems alone, but on individuals willing to retain moral authorship even when doing so is costly. When societies encourage that courage, they endure. When they punish it, they decay. There is no technological substitute for conscience. There is no administrative shortcut to morality. And there is no future in which sovereignty can be preserved without individuals willing to say no. That is not ideology.
