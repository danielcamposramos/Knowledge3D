When Accounting Meets Reality: Reflections On &quot;Alternative Energy Solutions At Scale&quot; harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in When Accounting Meets Reality: Reflections On &quot;Alternative Energy Solutions At Scale&quot; Energy, Fragility and the Cost of Pretending harry blazer Feb 07, 2026 4 2 3 Share For many years, I have struggled to understand the enthusiasm for so-called alternative energy at scale. Vast solar farms carpeting deserts and farmland. Endless rows of wind turbines marching across ridgelines and seas. Electric vehicles heralded as salvation. Ethanol blended into fuel as if plants could repeal thermodynamics. It was never a political objection. It was an accounting objection. When energy systems are evaluated honestly — not in isolation, not by press release, but by total embedded energy — the story changes. Materials must be mined. Components refined. Systems manufactured, transported, installed, maintained, replaced and eventually dismantled. Every step consumes energy before a single useful unit is delivered. When those costs are counted, something uncomfortable appears: without continuous subsidies, mandates and regulatory scaffolding, none of these systems stand on their own. That alone should provoke caution. But the deeper problem runs further than economics. The problem is fragility. The Collapse of Redundancy Modern energy enthusiasm is obsessed with efficiency, optimization and scale. What it quietly discards in the process is redundancy — the one trait that separates resilient systems from brittle ones. Traditional energy systems were not elegant. They were often dirty, inefficient and imperfect. But they were redundant . Failure in one region rarely meant instantaneous failure everywhere. Fuel could be stockpiled. Dispatch could be adjusted. Mechanical systems degraded gradually rather than catastrophically. Large-scale renewable systems invert this logic. They are centralized. They are synchronized. They are margin-thin. And they are deeply dependent on favorable conditions continuing to occur. This is not an abstract concern. It is already visible in practice. A single calm week can starve wind generation across entire regions. Smoke from distant wildfires can collapse solar output over thousands of square miles. Snow, dust or inverter failure can remove capacity instantly. And increasingly, a single hailstorm can wipe out a solar installation entirely — not partially, not gradually, but in one violent afternoon. This is not just damage. It is exposure. A system that loses years of embodied energy to one unexceptional weather event is not robust. It is over-optimized for a theoretical world that does not exist . Nature does not care about our models. Energy That Barely Clears the Bar Once fragility is visible, the deeper accounting problem comes into focus: net energy . Every civilization depends not on gross energy production, but on surplus energy — what remains after energy has paid for its own extraction and delivery. This surplus is what funds everything else: food systems, medicine, transportation, science, culture, defense and governance. “What are commonly called ‘fossil fuels’ — more accurately described here as extracted fuels — are dense, dispatchable chemical energy sources drawn from the Earth’s crust.” Historically, high-density extracted fuels provided extraordinary surplus. Early oil returned dozens — even hundreds — of units of energy for every unit invested. That surplus made modern complexity possible. Most alternative energy systems operate perilously close to the break-even line. Solar and wind, even under favorable assumptions, produce far less surplus once full lifecycles are counted. Add intermittency. Add storage. Add grid stabilization. Add replacement cycles. The margin shrinks much further. In some cases, it vanishes or goes negative. Ethanol is the clearest example. When fertilizers, machinery, transport, fermentation, distillation and land degradation are included, the energy returned barely exceeds the energy invested — if it exceeds it at all. This is not fuel. It is political choreography. Electric vehicles are usually spared this scrutiny. They should not be. The Cost of Pretending Conversions Don’t Matter Every conversion of energy from one state to another involves waste. This is not a controversial claim. It is the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Once that is acknowledged, the logic of electric vehicles charged from fossil-based electricity becomes difficult to defend. If an extracted fuel is burned to generate electricity, which is then transmitted, converted, stored chemically in a battery, reconverted to electricity and finally turned into mechanical motion, each step leaks energy. Always. By contrast, using that same fuel directly in an internal combustion engine involves fewer conversion steps. Losses still occur — but there are fewer places for energy to disappear . Which leads to an uncomfortable but unavoidable conclusion: If extracted fuel is the primary energy source, burning it once to propel a vehicle is more efficient than burning it to move electrons that then move the vehicle. The usual rebuttal is familiar: “But electric motors are more efficient than internal combustion engines.” True — at the motor . But systems don’t run on motors in isolation. They run on chains . System efficiency is multiplicative, not additive. A very efficient final step cannot redeem a long, lossy upstream pipeline. This is the same fallacy that makes people think: LED lighting “saves energy” even when rebound effects * erase the gain Cloud computing is “lighter” than local hardware Centralization always beats proximity In each case, attention is fixed on local efficiency while global loss is ignored . Electric vehicles, under fossil-dominated grids, do not represent an energy advance. They represent a re-routing of combustion — moving it upstream, out of sight and into more complex systems. Electric vehicles are not an energy solution. They are a conversion ritual designed to make combustion invisible . Demand, Dispatch and the Illusion of Storage There is one constraint that overrides all others in an electrical grid: supply must match demand in real time . Electricity is not like fuel. It cannot be stockpiled at scale in any economically meaningful way. Whatever is generated must be used immediately. If generation exceeds demand, frequency rises and systems destabilize. If generation falls short, frequency drops and protection systems trip. Either way, the result is the same: outages propagate. This is why demand fluctuation matters more than nameplate capacity. Wind and solar do not respond to demand. They respond to weather. Clouds do not wait for evening peaks. Wind does not accelerate when factories start their shifts. These systems produce when they produce — and stop when they stop. Because of this, every large-scale renewable installation must be paired with fast-dispatchable backup generation capable of ramping up and down instantly. In practice, that backup is almost always natural gas. The result is a paradox rarely acknowledged: the more intermittent capacity added to the grid, the more conventional capacity must remain online , synchronized, fueled, staffed and ready — even if it is running inefficiently or idling. This backup is not optional. It is not transitional. It is permanent. Energy surplus from wind or solar cannot be stored at grid scale without massive losses, prohibitive cost or both. Batteries capable of stabilizing national or regional grids for hours or days do not exist in economically viable form. Pumped hydro is geographically limited and already largely exploited. Hydrogen introduces additional conversion losses and infrastructure complexity that worsen the problem rather than solve it. As a result, renewable output must be managed in real time: If too much power is produced, generation must be curtailed — literally thrown away. If too little is produced, conventional generators must instantly fill the gap. This is not a flaw in implementation. It is a structural reality. Grids fail not because average production is insufficient, but because peaks and troughs are misaligned . Starvation and overload — not annual totals — are what bring systems down. When failures occur, they cascade rapidly across large geographic areas because modern grids are tightly coupled and highly interdependent. In this context, intermittent energy does not replace conventional generation. It rides on top of it , parasitically dependent on a fully maintained system it claims to make obsolete. What is being built is not a new energy system, but a more complex, less efficient and more failure-prone version of the old one — with fewer margins and more points of collapse. The Environmental Cost That Doesn’t Get Counted The final irony is that these systems are promoted as environmentally virtuous while imposing new and often permanent environmental damage . Industrial wind installations are not passive structures. They alter airflow, fragment habitats and kill birds and bats at scale — including protected and migratory species. These losses are not hypothetical. They are documented, persistent and dismissed as acceptable collateral. Large solar farms are not benign fields of glass. They require massive land clearing, grading, fencing and access roads. They disrupt soil ecology, alter drainage patterns, displace wildlife and convert living landscapes into industrial zones. Once installed, the land is rarely restored to its prior state. Biofuels are worse. Corn ethanol requires monoculture agriculture at industrial scale. This means heavy fertilizer use, pesticide and herbicide runoff, soil depletion, aquifer stress and biological simplification. Genetically modified corn grown for fuel intensifies every one of these pressures — degrading soil, contaminating water, affecting air quality, collapsing local biodiversity and changing biomes at scale in the external world and internally in the bodies of humans and animals — all to produce a fuel that barely clears the energy break-even line. None of this is incidental. It is structural. Even aesthetics matter. Wind turbines permanently alter landscapes, skylines and horizons. What was once open, rural or wild becomes industrial — potentially forever. These are not temporary interventions. They are generational transformations imposed in the name of sustainability. What emerges is not environmental harmony, but environmental tradeoffs that are rarely admitted and never fully counted . When the Accounting Becomes Inconvenient This dynamic is not new. It has played out before — quietly, and with remarkably little public memory. When corn ethanol was first being promoted as a large-scale fuel replacement, a small number of researchers subjected it to full energy accounting rather than policy modeling. Among them was Tad Patzek , whom I visited at the time at the University of California, Berkeley, whose work showed that once fertilizers, farm machinery, transport, fermentation and distillation were fully counted, corn ethanol was at best marginal — and in some analyses, a net energy loss. These findings were not fringe. They were grounded in physical inputs and thermodynamic constraints. They were also deeply inconvenient. The ethanol industry responded with counter-studies that relied on more generous system boundaries, optimistic assumptions and selective exclusions. The debate was not resolved by better accounting. It was resolved by narrative momentum. In 2007, BP announced a $500 million bioenergy research partnership with UC Berkeley and affiliated institutions — one of the largest single corporate research investments in the university’s history. The initiative reframed biofuels as a frontier of innovation and promise. What followed was not an explicit silencing, but something more subtle and more common: attention moved . Funding priorities shifted. Certain questions stopped being asked. Lines of inquiry that challenged the premise of large-scale biofuels faded from institutional focus. Patzek eventually left Berkeley. The ethanol narrative continued largely unimpeded. Modern research ecosystems respond to incentives. When a line of inquiry threatens a multi-billion-dollar policy and investment trajectory, it does not need to be banned. It only needs to become unfundable . This is how inconvenient physics is often handled — not by refutation, but by neglect. Why Centralization Makes Everything Worse Low-density energy sources require massive infrastructure to compensate for their diffuseness. That infrastructure must be centralized to achieve scale. Centralization concentrates risk. A coal plant can operate day and night regardless of clouds or calm air. A nuclear plant delivers power in storms, heat waves and winters alike. Their vulnerabilities exist — but they are narrow and well understood. By contrast, centralized renewable installations share the same exposure simultaneously. When conditions fail, they fail together. When damage occurs, it occurs across entire fleets. Recovery is slow, expensive and energy-intensive. This is not how resilient systems are built. It is how fragile systems are optimized on paper . The irony is painful: systems promoted as environmentally harmonious are often the least tolerant of the environment as it actually behaves. A technology that cannot survive hail, smoke, cold, calm air, drought or seasonal variability is not aligned with nature. It is aligned with a spreadsheet. The Unspoken Driver: Declining Surplus There is a reason these contradictions are tolerated. We are living through a period of declining net energy availability. The easy surplus that once underwrote prosperity is shrinking. As surplus declines, societies attempt to preserve appearances through complexity, narrative and policy. Subsidies expand. Accounting becomes selective. Moral language replaces engineering language. Dissent is framed as denial rather than analysis. Instead of admitting that modern civilization was built on a one-time energy windfall, we pretend that diffuse, intermittent sources can simply substitute for dense, dispatchable ones — if only we believe hard enough. But belief does not raise EROEI (energy return on energy invested) The Moral Substitution Error The final error is philosophical. We have attempted to replace energy density with moral intention . Extracted fuels are not powerful because they are immoral. They are powerful because they are energy dense, portable and reliable. No amount of ethical framing changes that fact. Condemning density does not create alternatives with equivalent surplus. When moral narratives are used to suppress accounting, systems fail silently — until they fail loudly. A society that confuses intention with capacity eventually discovers the difference the hard way. Reality Always Collects the Bill The hailstorm matters because it is honest. It does not argue. It does not negotiate. It simply reveals whether a system was built with margin or fantasy. Energy systems that only function under ideal conditions are not solutions. They are liabilities deferred into the future. Physics does not care what we meant to do. It only cares what works. If we want resilience, we must design for reality, not virtue. If we want sustainability, we must start with surplus, not slogans. And if we want honesty, we must count everything — even when we don’t like the answer. Addendum: Efficiency, Rebound Effects and the Myth of Energy Savings A recurring assumption in modern energy policy is that improving efficiency naturally leads to lower total energy use. The intuition seems obvious: if each unit of activity consumes less energy, aggregate consumption should fall. In practice, this assumption is often false. *This phenomenon is known as the rebound effect , and in its strongest form, it has been recognized for more than a century. In the 19th century, the economist William Stanley Jevons observed that improvements in the efficiency of coal use did not reduce coal consumption in England. Instead, they made coal cheaper to use, expanded its applications, and increased total demand. This dynamic has since been referred to as Jevons Paradox . The underlying mechanism is straightforward and does not depend on ideology or psychology. When a technology becomes more efficient: The cost per unit of service declines That service becomes more accessible Usage expands in scope, duration, or intensity The system scales to absorb — and often exceed — the efficiency gain Efficiency increases capacity , not restraint. . LEDs as a Modern Case Study Light-emitting diode (LED) technology is a textbook example. At the device level, LEDs are genuinely efficient. They produce more light per watt, generate less heat at the emitter and promise longer lifespans under ideal conditions. Measured narrowly, they “save energy.” At the system level, however, the outcome is different. Because lighting becomes cheaper to operate: More lights are installed Lights are left on longer Outdoor and decorative lighting proliferates Illumination spreads into spaces that were previously dark or minimally lit Satellite observations over the past several decades show that global nighttime brightness has increased substantially, even as lighting efficiency has improved. The efficiency gain lowered the cost of illumination, and illumination expanded to fill the new margin. This is rebound in its pure form : the energy saved per unit is offset — and in many cases overwhelmed — by increased total use. Rebound Is Not the Same as Embedded Cost It is important to distinguish rebound effects from other legitimate critiques of “efficiency” technologies. For example, there are several additional factors associated with LEDs: Energy-intensive semiconductor manufacturing Globalized supply chains and materials extraction Disposal challenges and electronic waste The legacy problem of mercury contamination from compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs), which were widely promoted as an efficiency solution before LEDs The possibility that electronically produced light may have negative implications for human health These are embedded and lifecycle costs , not rebound effects. They matter greatly, but they are conceptually separate. Rebound occurs after efficiency is achieved. Embedded cost occurs before efficiency is realized. In many modern systems, both operate simultaneously — compounding the error. Why Rebound Is Systemic, Not Accidental Rebound effects persist because modern systems are optimized for: Growth Convenience Expansion Utilization of available capacity They are not optimized for sufficiency. When efficiency lowers operating costs, market and institutional pressures push systems to scale. This is not greed or malice. It is how competitive systems behave when constraints are relaxed. Absent hard limits — cultural, regulatory or physical — efficiency improvements tend to accelerate throughput rather than reduce it. This pattern repeats across domains: Transportation efficiency encourages more travel Data compression encourages more data creation Computing efficiency encourages more computation Energy efficiency encourages more energy services Efficiency without constraint is not conservation. It is amplification . Why This Matters for Energy Policy Rebound effects explain why many energy transitions disappoint even when technologies “work as designed.” They also explain why: Efficiency gains fail to reduce total emissions Energy demand continues to rise despite technical improvements Complexity increases rather than stabilizes Margins shrink while dependencies multiply Most importantly, rebound effects expose the category error at the heart of modern energy thinking: We treat efficiency as a substitute for limits. It is not. Efficiency changes how fast a system can operate. It does not decide how far that system will go. The Deeper Implication Rebound effects are uncomfortable because they imply something most policy frameworks avoid: Real energy reduction is not primarily a technical problem. It is a systems and governance problem. Without explicit boundaries — on scale, throughput,\ and dependency — efficiency gains will be absorbed by expansion until physical limits reassert themselves in harsher ways. This is why counting matters. This is why margins matter. And this is why narratives that celebrate efficiency while ignoring system behavior consistently fail. Physics does not reward good intentions. It responds only to totals. THE MOST RECENT CASE STUDY - FINLAND - AND ITS RAMIFICATIONS FOR THOSE WITH EYES OPEN When Winter Arrives Finland, Wind Power and the Cost of Agenda-Driven Energy For years, Finland has been held up as a model of climate responsibility — a technologically advanced nation leading Europe’s decarbonization push, boasting one of the highest shares of renewable energy in final consumption across the EU. The narrative was clear: this is what a clean, modern energy future looks like. Then winter arrived. A prolonged cold snap pushed temperatures well below freezing across western Finland, where most of the country’s wind generation capacity is concentrated. Low fog clouds formed at turbine height. Ice accumulated on blades. Wind output collapsed. According to grid operator Fingrid Oyj , wind generation fell from roughly 9,400 MW to about 430 MW — barely 5% of installed capacity. Turbines were shut down to prevent damage. Output is expected to remain far below normal for weeks. Electricity prices spiked to the highest levels of the winter. Working households absorbed the shock. And in a grimly ironic turn, many Finns turned to wood stoves and fireplaces to stay warm — in a country praised for having transcended such “primitive” solutions. This was not an accident. It was not unforeseeable. And it was not a failure of technology alone. It was a failure of clear thinking . A Failure Under Guaranteed Conditions Is Not a Surprise Finland is not unfamiliar with cold. It is not a developing nation. It is not lacking engineering talent, grid expertise or institutional capacity. If wind turbines freeze in Finland, the problem is not that winter was unexpected. It is that systems were deployed as symbols rather than as infrastructure . An energy source that collapses precisely when demand peaks — during cold, dark winter conditions — is not a supply system. It is a dependency. Wind does not respond to human demand. It responds to weather. And when weather diverges from need, physics wins. This failure mode was obvious from the beginning. Intermittency, Demand and the Myth of Storage Electric grids require real-time balance. Supply must match demand continuously. Electricity cannot be stockpiled at scale in any economically meaningful way. Wind and solar do not follow demand. They produce when conditions allow. When output exceeds demand, power must be curtailed — wasted. When output collapses, dispatchable generation must immediately fill the gap. That backup is not optional. It is permanent. In practice, this means natural gas, nuclear, or hydro must remain fully available, synchronized, staffed, fueled and ready — even if policy claims they are being “phased out.” Wind does not replace dispatchable power. It rides on top of it. Finland’s winter simply exposed the dependency. The Conversion Chain Problem Every conversion of energy from one state to another involves loss. This is not ideology. It is thermodynamics. Wind power introduces: mechanical conversion electrical conversion transmission losses curtailment losses backup inefficiencies These losses are acceptable only if surplus energy remains high. It does not. When margins are thin, losses matter. When demand spikes, they become catastrophic. Environmental Tradeoffs That Don’t Disappear Wind power is often defended as environmentally benign. Finland’s experience highlights the flaw in that assumption. Wind installations: fragment habitats alter airflow and microclimates kill birds and bats at scale require massive material inputs impose permanent landscape changes And in cold climates, they require additional infrastructure — blade heating systems, maintenance access, redundancy — all of which reduce net energy return further. None of this disappears because a technology is labeled “green.” The Rebound and Fragility Trap Efficiency does not equal resilience. As wind capacity expanded, confidence increased. Redundancy was quietly reduced. Dependence deepened. When the system failed, it failed system-wide, driving price spikes and social stress. This is the rebound effect at the grid level: efficiency and scale invite over-reliance, which erodes safety margins until a shock exposes fragility. Agenda-Driven Science, Not Surprise None of this was unknowable. Scientists, engineers and grid experts have raised these objections for decades: intermittency demand mismatch low net energy storage infeasibility environmental displacement fragility under extremes These objections were not refuted. They were overridden. This was not a spontaneous market outcome. It was a coordinated policy agenda — legislated, subsidized and institutionally reinforced. Preferred outcomes were selected in advance. Research funding, regulatory structures and public messaging followed. Dissent did not need to be censored. It merely needed to become unfundable. This is how modern agendas enforce themselves: not through secrecy, but through normalization. Why This Matters Beyond Energy Finland’s frozen turbines are not just an energy story. They are a pattern . The same structure is now visible in biotechnology and AI: deployment outruns understanding scale substitutes for proof risks are deferred critics are labeled obstructionist “inevitability” replaces judgment Energy policy showed us what happens when science stops saying “no.” AI and biotech are following the same trajectory — with far higher stakes. The Real Lesson of Winter Finland did not fail because it lacked intelligence or technology. It failed because ideology displaced engineering . When systems are designed to satisfy narratives rather than reality, failure is only a matter of timing. Winter arrived on schedule. Physics responded on cue. The lesson is simple and brutal: When a system fails under conditions it was guaranteed to encounter, the failure is not accidental — it is ideological. And no amount of moral language can keep people warm when the lights go out. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
