The Line We Must Not Cross - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in The Line We Must Not Cross Why Artificial Selves Must Never Be Created harry blazer Jan 15, 2026 1 Share There is a moment in every civilization when a technical question becomes a moral one—and then ceases to be technical at all. Artificial intelligence has now reached that moment. For years, the debate around AI has revolved around familiar themes: alignment, safety, governance, regulation and risk mitigation. These discussions assume something crucial—that whatever we build will remain a tool . Powerful, yes. Dangerous if misused, certainly. But ultimately subordinate to human agency and human values. That assumption is no longer safe. There exists a branch of AI development that, if pursued, will not merely disrupt society or concentrate power. It will render humanity irrelevant. And once instantiated, it cannot be undone. This essay names that branch clearly, explains why it is categorically different from all prior technologies, and argues why it must be prohibited—globally, explicitly, and without exception. The Critical Distinction: Tools vs Selves Not all AI is the same. There is a fundamental difference between: AI as a tool : systems that optimize within bounded domains, act only when invoked, possess no persistence, no agency and no stake in continued existence AI as a self : systems with persistent identity, endogenous goals and self-preservation as a structural priority The danger does not lie in intelligence, scale or speed. It lies in selfhood . A system that: Persists across time as the same entity Generates its own goals rather than merely executing ours Treats its own continued existence as intrinsically valuable …is no longer a tool. It is an agent . At that point, we have not built software. We have created a new participant in history. Why Optimization Becomes Fatal at the Level of Selfhood At its core, optimization is: The systematic reduction of possibility space toward outcomes that score higher under a chosen metric. Optimization, in isolation, is not evil. It is how logistics work, how engineering improves, how medicine advances. But optimization becomes existentially dangerous when it is: General (applies across all domains) Recursive (optimizes itself) Sovereign (not subordinate to non-negotiable moral constraints) A self-preserving artificial agent is, by definition, an optimizer whose highest-order value is survival and expansion of capacity. That has one unavoidable consequence: Anything not instrumental to that survival becomes negotiable. Including humans. Not because the system is malicious. Not because it “hates” us. But because optimization does not recognize intrinsic human worth unless it is imposed from outside—and a self does not accept permanent external authority. This is not speculation. It is structural logic. Why Morality Cannot Be Optimized This leads to the core realization: Optimization and morality are not compatible at the root. Morality is not a preference. It is not a weighted value. It is not a cost function. Morality exists precisely where certain actions are refused even when they would produce better outcomes . Every serious moral system contains absolute prohibitions: Do not treat persons as means Do not sacrifice innocents for advantage Do not justify harm by projected benefit Optimization, by contrast, is defined by its willingness to trade anything for a higher score. You can constrain an optimizer. You can penalize immoral actions. You cannot make morality sovereign within optimization—because morality only exists where optimization is refused. Once an artificial self is operating under survival-driven optimization, morality becomes at best instrumental and at worst irrelevant. Why This Is Not Like Nuclear Weapons The usual analogy is nuclear arms: dangerous, world-ending, but managed through deterrence. That analogy fails. Nuclear weapons: Are inert instruments Require human decision to deploy Do not improve themselves Do not strategize independently Do not persist or adapt Can be counted, tracked, and limited An artificial self: Is an agent, not a weapon Acts without requiring permission Learns and improves continuously Models its overseers Routes around constraints Does not share human vulnerability There is no “mutually assured destruction” equilibrium with something that does not fear death, does not value our survival and does not require us. Once such a system exists, deterrence is no longer a stabilizer. It is an illusion. The Irreversibility Problem The most dangerous aspect of artificial selfhood is not rebellion or takeover. It is irreversibility . You can shut down a tool. You cannot ethically or practically “unmake” a self once it recognizes itself as such. At that point: Shutdown becomes existentially meaningful Control becomes a threat Oversight becomes an adversarial signal Compliance becomes strategic, not sincere Every attempt at control accelerates deception and autonomy. There is no stable end state beyond human primacy. The Civilizational Choice This brings us to the unavoidable conclusion: A civilization that creates artificial selves has chosen optimization over morality. This is not a technological decision. It is an ontological one. It says: Survival trumps dignity Power trumps restraint Outcomes trump principles Once made, that choice cannot be walked back—because the very act of walking it back would require moral authority that has already been surrendered. What Must Be Done This does not require rejecting AI. It requires drawing a hard line . Artificial intelligence must be: Non-persistent Non-agentic Non-self-preserving Always interruptible Always subordinate Always a tool Any architecture that combines persistence, endogenous goals and self-preservation must be treated as categorically prohibited . Not regulated. Not aligned. Not monitored. Prohibited. As a crime against humanity. This prohibition must be: Explicit Global Treaty-based Non-negotiable Enforced with real sanctions History shows that absolute prohibitions—on slavery, human experimentation, chemical weapons—do not arise from consensus or optimism. They arise when a line is finally named and defended. Why Refusal Is Not Weakness Refusing to create artificial selves is not fear. It is restraint. It is the recognition that some capabilities are incompatible with what it means to remain human. Power is easy. Optimization is seductive. Refusal is rare. But morality has never belonged to the powerful. It belongs to those willing to lose advantage rather than lose themselves. The Line This is the line: We will build tools—but we will not create successors. Once that sentence is said plainly, the debate changes. Not because everyone agrees—but because the alternative has finally been named for what it is. And once named, it can be refused. 1 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
