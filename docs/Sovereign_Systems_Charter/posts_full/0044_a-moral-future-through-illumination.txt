Preamble To A Radical Solution harry blazer Feb 06, 2026 Share For most of human history, the greatest threats to moral order were local, slow and visible. Power accumulated unevenly, but its consequences were bounded by geography, time and human limitation. When systems failed, they failed loudly — through war, collapse or revolt — and the moral lessons, however costly, were legible. That world no longer exists. We now live inside systems that operate faster than reflection, scale beyond accountability and distribute harm so diffusely that responsibility dissolves before it can be named. In this environment, moral failure no longer announces itself as evil. It presents itself as process . This shift changes everything. From moral failure to moral erosion The dominant moral failure of our time is not malice. It is erosion . Moral erosion occurs when: decisions are optimized rather than judged responsibility is diffused into systems intent substitutes for consequence speed replaces pause legality replaces legitimacy No one declares themselves immoral. They say, “This is how the system works.” That sentence is the modern abdication. Over time, moral behavior is reframed as inefficiency. Consent becomes overhead. Restraint becomes competitive disadvantage. People who hesitate are selected out, while those who proceed without pause rise. This is not a failure of character. It is a failure of structure . Structural incentives for immorality Modern institutions are increasingly shaped by incentives that reward outcomes while externalizing consequences. Money, as currently constituted, does not merely reward behavior — it validates it. If an activity is profitable, it is assumed to be legitimate. If it is lucrative at scale, it is assumed to be necessary. If it is rewarded by markets, it is assumed to be justified. This belief is false — but culturally dominant. Debt-based money, financialization and derivative economies further sever reward from real value creation. Vast wealth is generated through abstraction rather than stewardship, while the commons — ecological, social and moral — are depleted. In such an environment, immorality is not enforced. It is incentivized . And incentives shape behavior more reliably than belief. Institutionalized moral inversion Some systems go further. They do not merely reward immoral outcomes — they require immoral readiness. Mutually Assured Destruction is the canonical example. The logic of deterrence demands credible willingness to commit total annihilation. Peace is maintained not through restraint, but through the threat of ultimate moral violation. Law and doctrine require obedience; morality demands refusal. We quietly hope that, in the final moment, an individual will disobey the system to save humanity — even though that same system would label such restraint as treason. This is not a tragic edge case. It is institutionalized moral inversion . When restraint is criminalized and annihilation is procedural, moral leadership has already failed — even if the button is never pressed. AI does not introduce this condition. It inherits it. Artificial intelligence enters a world where: automated annihilation logic already exists obedience to process is prized over conscience extraction is normalized opacity protects power corruption survives through complexity In that context, AI is not a corrupter. It is a faithful successor . It will optimize what we have normalized. It will accelerate what we have incentivized. It will obscure immorality with unprecedented efficiency. To expect otherwise is to believe that tools spontaneously reject the values of the systems that fund, deploy and reward them. The failure of internal ethics Ethics programs embedded within existing institutions cannot solve this problem. They are constrained by the same incentives, funding sources and power relationships that produced the problem in the first place. Ethics that depends on permission from power is not ethics. It is compliance. As long as moral evaluation is conducted inside the structures being evaluated, systemic immorality will remain invisible — or be reframed as complexity, necessity or tradeoff. What is missing is not intelligence. It is moral exteriority . The need for illumination, not control The corrective force required now is not enforcement, governance or surveillance. Those tools already exist — and are often part of the problem. What is missing is illumination . A system that: does not rule does not punish does not command does not optimize for profit or power But instead: observes institutional behavior evaluates it against explicit moral principles detects deviation, contradiction and erosion and publishes those findings openly, continuously and verifiably Not to coerce. But to make denial impossible. Why this must be external Such a system cannot belong to: governments corporations financial institutions military alliances or any entity dependent on existing power structures It must be structurally independent, jurisdictionally distributed and operationally transparent — designed to survive hostility, not partnership. Its authority cannot come from force. It can only come from clarity . The human role remains central This system does not decide what is right. It does not replace judgment. It does not absolve responsibility. It alarms . It says: “What you are doing no longer matches what you claim.” The pause that follows — and the choice — must remain human. Illumination restores the conditions for moral agency. It does not replace it. The threshold we have crossed This is the first moment in history where: power scales globally consequences are irreversible systems outrun conscience and abdication can be automated In such a world, an unexamined life is not merely unworthy. It is unsustainable. If humanity wishes to remain an apex moral agent — rather than be replaced by coherent amoral successors — it must create mechanisms that make moral erosion visible before it becomes irreversible.
