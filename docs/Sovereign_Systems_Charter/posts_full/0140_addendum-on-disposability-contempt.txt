It rests on language —the language of abstraction and expendability. One of the most widely circulated quotations attributed to Kissinger is: “Military men are just dumb, stupid animals to be used as pawns in foreign policy.” This remark is commonly traced to a 1969 conversation reported by journalist Oriana Fallaci . Kissinger later disputed the phrasing, claiming exaggeration. What matters, however, is not the contested sentence alone, but the consistency of outlook visible across his documented statements and actions. What is not disputed is Kissinger’s explicit willingness to accept mass civilian and military casualties as a legitimate instrument of policy. In defending the secret bombing of Cambodia, he stated in a 1975 interview: “The illegal we do immediately; the unconstitutional takes a little longer.” That sentence is not rhetorical flourish. It is a worldview. Within that worldview, soldiers are not moral agents but deployable variables —necessary, tragic and ultimately expendable. The system does not hate them. It simply does not see them. That is colder than hatred. Dick Cheney and the Normalization of Permanent War If Kissinger represents technocratic abstraction, Dick Cheney represents institutional ruthlessness—war not as tragic necessity, but as enduring condition . Cheney was explicit that the so-called “War on Terror” would not end, and should not be constrained by traditional moral or legal limits. In a 2001 interview on Meet the Press , he stated: “We’re going to have to work sort of the dark side, if you will… a lot of what needs to be done here will have to be done quietly, without any discussion.” This was not said in ignorance of consequence. It was said in full awareness that secrecy dissolves accountability. As to war crimes: Cheney was a principal architect of policies involving: Indefinite detention Torture euphemized as “enhanced interrogation” Wars of aggression justified by claims later shown to be false Whether or not courts ever act, the evidentiary record is no longer in serious dispute . 9/11 and the Question of Complicity “ Crossing the Rubicon” by Mike Ruppert itself represents a Rubicon crossing as well. Ruppert documents meticulously: Prior intelligence warnings Financial foreknowledge (insider trading anomalies) Structural stand-downs Simultaneous War Games to confuse and distract The immediate policy exploitation of the attacks From this, Ruppert argues complicity by omission, obstruction and foreknowledge , not cartoon villainy. Whether one accepts his ultimate conclusions or not i.e. Cheney’s complicity in and facilitation of 9-11 - a Secret Team OP -, the evidence he assembled has never been fully answered—only ignored . That silence is itself revealing. George W. Bush and the Face of Moral Vacancy As for George W. Bush , history may remember him not as a mastermind but as something arguably more dangerous: a moral null at the center of enormous destructive power. This is the man who, when informed on September 11 that the nation was under attack, continued reading The Pet Goat to children for several minutes. This is the man who later joked publicly about searching for weapons of mass destruction under Oval Office furniture. Evil does not always announce itself with genius. Sometimes it arrives as cheerful vacancy coupled to lethal authority . Why This Matters What unites Kissinger, Cheney and Bush is not ideology, party or temperament. It is distance from consequence . They authorized violence. Others absorbed it. And when the accounting came due, they spoke in abstractions—or not at all. To call this out is not radical. It is not conspiratorial. It is not hateful. It is moral memory . And moral memory is precisely what systems of power depend upon citizens losing. 3 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
