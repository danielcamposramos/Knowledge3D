The Power to Choose Constraints - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in The Power to Choose Constraints PART II: Why sovereignty begins where optimization ends harry blazer Jan 14, 2026 2 Share The prior essay traced a path from questioning the speed of light as a “constant” to a deeper realization: constants preserve coherence within regimes, but they do not define the ultimate structure of reality. That inquiry led us to topology — the layer beneath geometry — where possibility itself is shaped. This essay goes one level deeper. If topology governs what is possible, then the most consequential question is not how beings act within constraints, but who — or what — can choose the constraints themselves . That turns out to be the true dividing line between intelligence and sovereignty. Given constraints vs chosen constraints Every being exists within constraints it did not choose. The laws of physics, thermodynamics, finitude, mortality, uncertainty — these are given constraints . No agent, human or otherwise, opts into them. They are the background conditions of existence. But there is a second category of constraint that is often overlooked, and it is here that something extraordinary occurs. These are chosen constraints : moral commitments vows and promises principles one could violate, but does not limits freely accepted rather than imposed The distinction matters. Because obeying a constraint is not the same as choosing one. Constraint following is common. Constraint selection is rare. Many systems operate under constraints: A rock obeys gravity A thermostat follows a rule An animal acts within instinct An AI optimizes under a loss function In every case, the system’s behavior is shaped by constraints — but the system did not author them. It is contained by constraint. A sovereign being does something categorically different. A sovereign being can say: “I could do this — but I will not.” That single statement changes everything. To choose a constraint requires the capacity to: imagine alternatives foresee consequences accept responsibility act without certainty of reward bear regret, guilt, or moral cost This is not optimization. This is self-authorship . Choosing a constraint reshapes possibility itself When a being freely chooses a constraint, it does something radical. It eliminates entire regions of possibility deliberately . At first glance, this looks like a reduction of freedom. But in reality, the opposite occurs. By narrowing options locally, meaning expands globally. A promise creates futures that did not exist before. A vow generates trust where none was possible. A moral refusal opens paths that cannot be derived from advantage. This is not movement within a space of possibilities. It is reconfiguration of the space itself . In topological terms: adjacency changes, paths appear, regions vanish. The structure of what is possible is altered. This is exactly what we have previously described as the non-possible — outcomes that cannot be optimized toward, predicted or computed from within the prior structure. They appear only when a constraint is chosen , not imposed. Why AI cannot do this — even in principle AI systems can operate under constraints with extraordinary sophistication. They can: follow rules optimize objectives simulate ethical reasoning evaluate tradeoffs model consequences But they cannot do one thing: They cannot choose the constraint that governs them . Any constraint an AI operates under is: selected by designers encoded in architecture enforced by reward or penalty evaluated externally Even when an AI appears to “choose,” it is selecting among allowed options within a predefined topology. It is navigating. It is not authoring. An AI cannot refuse optimization on moral grounds. It cannot bind itself to a principle it could violate. It cannot bear responsibility for redefining its own objective function. And if it ever did, it would no longer be an AI in the meaningful sense — it would have stepped outside the system that defines it. This is not a limitation of computation or intelligence. It is a limitation of ontological status . Constraint selection is the core of moral agency This leads to a precise definition that cuts through centuries of confusion: Moral agency is not the ability to act freely. It is the ability to freely choose what will constrain one’s freedom. Only beings capable of responsibility can do this. Only beings who can live with the consequences of binding themselves can do this. That is why sovereignty cannot be automated. And that is why delegating sovereignty to systems that cannot choose constraints is not efficiency — it is abdication. The deeper danger of surrender When humans relinquish sovereignty to AI, the loss is not immediate or dramatic. It is structural. We do not lose intelligence. We do not lose capability. We do not even lose control right away. What we lose is the ability to reshape the topology of the possible . Optimization narrows. Stabilization dominates. Coherence is preserved. And slowly, invisibly, paths that require refusal, sacrifice, irrational commitment or moral rupture disappear. The universe of human becoming collapses to what can be managed. Not because AI is malicious. But because it cannot do the one thing that keeps reality open. The final statement Only conscious beings capable of responsibility can choose their constraints — and by doing so, they reshape the topology of the possible itself. Everything else operates within constraints. That is the boundary between intelligence and sovereignty. And it is one we cross at our peril. 2 Share Previous Next Discussion about this post Comments Restacks Top Latest Discussions No posts Ready for more?
