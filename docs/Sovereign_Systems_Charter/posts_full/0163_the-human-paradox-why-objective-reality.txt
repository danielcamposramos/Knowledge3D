The Human Paradox: Why Objective Reality Exists, Why Humans Can’t Be Averaged, and Why This Matters in a World Tilting Toward Digital Control harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in The Human Paradox: Why Objective Reality Exists, Why Humans Can’t Be Averaged, and Why This Matters in a World Tilting Toward Digital Control harry blazer Dec 13, 2025 Share There is a strange idea floating around today that everything is a construct, that reality is a hallucination we agree to share, that there is no objective world outside the stories we tell about it. This kind of subjectivism has become fashionable in academia, popular culture and even certain corners of spirituality. But the moment someone declares “there is no objective reality,” they immediately rely on objective reality to make the claim. They sit on a chair that must hold their weight. They trust their brakes when they drive. They avoid bullets, cliffs, toxins and oncoming cars. They speak sentences they expect others to understand. They act as if truth exists, even when their philosophy denies it. This is the performative contradiction at the heart of radical subjectivism: You can only deny objective reality by depending on it. Perceptions are indeed constructed. But constraints are not. Gravity doesn’t negotiate with your worldview. Poison doesn’t check your beliefs before entering your bloodstream. A wall does not dissolve because you theorize it. A jury doesn’t decide “perspectives,” they decide facts. Bombs kill the guilty and innocent alike regardless of interpretive frameworks. Human civilization is one massive bet that something outside us is real, stable and consequential . Yet, here is where things become fascinating. Even though we share a common world, human beings are not carbon copies. We are similar enough to live in the same reality, but different enough that any attempt to statistically average us—to treat us as interchangeable units—will always fail. Sometimes catastrophically. This is the paradox at the center of sovereignty. We See Enough of the Same World to Agree on Reality With rare exceptions, humans share: the same basic senses the same physics the same body plan the same evolutionary equipment This lets us say “that’s a car,” “that’s a cliff,” “that stove is hot” and “that explosion killed people.” Shared reality is what makes language, law, engineering, agriculture, science and morality possible. Without objective reality, meaning collapses. Without shared perception, communication collapses. Without invariant consequences, responsibility collapses. This is why nihilistic subjectivism is not just wrong. It’s dangerous. It is a gateway drug to governance through illusion. But We Are Different Enough That Uniform Solutions Break Us The deeper truth is this: Human beings are not uniform. We are not interchangeable. We are not reducible to averages. Immune systems differ wildly. Microbiomes are unique. Detox pathways vary by orders of magnitude. Genetic polymorphisms alter how we metabolize food, drugs, stress and trauma. Our minds, bodies, histories and nervous systems are individual, situated, analog. This is where centralized interventions fall apart. Vaccines assume uniformity in immune response. Psychiatric drugs assume uniformity in neurochemistry. Dietary guidelines assume uniformity in metabolism. Education assumes uniformity in cognition. Algorithmic governance assumes uniformity in behavior. Synthetic biology assumes uniformity in risk tolerance and biological fragility. “Population-level safety” is a mathematical fiction. The population does not exist. Only individuals do. The more a society depends on digital, industrial or algorithmic standardization, the more harm it does to the people who fall outside its confidence intervals. And that tail is not small. It grows with every layer of simplification. Digitization converges toward good enough while Humanity manifests the non-possible. But only as individuals. The Sovereignty Lesson Hidden in the Paradox This tension between shared reality and irreducible individuality is not an accident. It is the architecture of human existence. Sovereignty requires enough common ground to speak truth, and enough difference to make truth personal. Any system that ignores human similarity becomes incoherent. Any system that ignores human individuality becomes tyrannical. The modern world is sliding into the second category. Everything digital demands standardization. Everything analog resists it. Which brings us to the looming question. Does This Refute the Simulation Hypothesis? This paradox cuts directly into the heart of the simulation argument. A simulation requires: compressibility predictability uniform rules interchangeable agents tractable variability Humans violate all five. We share enough structure to render perception stable, but differ enough that no simple compression algorithm can predict our biology or behavior without massive loss. This doesn’t disprove simulation theory, but it does two important things: 1. It makes a simplistic simulation model impossible If reality were a video game, humans would be the worst-designed NPCs ever. Too complex to simulate cheaply, too variable to control consistently, too unpredictable to reduce to parameters. It shows that sovereignty, individuality and consciousness cannot be reduced to digital objects Even if we were inside some kind of simulation, the simulation must still be hosting analog-like singularities that cannot be averaged, compressed or replaced. Meaning: Your sovereignty is real either way. Your individuality is non-negotiable either way. Your responsibility remains intact either way. Reality may be deeper than our senses suggest. But it is not arbitrary, and it is not programmable by narrative. The Conclusion We live in a world that is objectively real enough to demand responsibility, yet individually variable enough that industrial and digital systems inevitably misfire when they attempt uniform control. This is not a flaw in humanity. It is the essence of humanity. It is why sovereignty exists. It is why freedom matters. It is why centralization always fails. It is why analog life cannot be captured by digital systems. It is why attempts to “average” humans will always break those at the margins. It is why statistical utopias inevitably become dystopias. It is why humanity manifests the non-possible. And it is why no simulation, no matter how sophisticated, can erase the analog fact of who we are. If there is a unified thesis here, it is this: Humans share a world, but not an identity. We inhabit the same reality, but not the same body. We play by the same physics, but not the same code.
