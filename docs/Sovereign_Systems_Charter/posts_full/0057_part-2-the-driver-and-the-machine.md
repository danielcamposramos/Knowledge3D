## Summary
The false binary: control vs surrender Public discourse often frames this issue incorrectly, as a binary choice: Either humans remain “in control,” Or machines take over. Execution domains (computational excellence) These are domains characterized by: Clearly defined objectives Formalizable constraints Measurable success criteria High dimensionality Severe time pressure Examples include: Optimal racing lines and vehicle dynamics Collision avoidance and emergency braking Target tracking and interception Game move selection (chess, Go) Pattern recognition in imaging Large-scale document analysis In these domains, machines outperform humans because they are better suited to the task. Judgment domains (normative authority) These are domains characterized by: Value tradeoffs Moral or ethical consequence Ambiguous or contested goals Novelty that breaks prior models (more on this soon) Irreversible harm The need for accountability Examples include: Whether lethal force should be deployed What level of risk is acceptable to impose on others How justice should be interpreted or balanced What constitutes appropriate medical care for a particular patient When to override or shut down an automated system Who bears responsibility when harm occurs These domains are not computational problems.

- A racing example (since this is where we started) Imagine an autonomous race system trained on: Clean track Known grip evolution Standard car behavior Now introduce novelty : Oil dropped mid-corner by another car A damaged suspension component A marshal standing in an unexpected location A tire delaminating in a way never seen in training A human driver may: Sense something “off” before identifying it Abort, lift, or choose caution without full understanding A machine will: Continue executing the optimal line Because nothing in its model says the world has changed That’s novelty invalidating the model.
- The question of authority in these systems is often overlooked but so fundamentl for ethical AI development.
