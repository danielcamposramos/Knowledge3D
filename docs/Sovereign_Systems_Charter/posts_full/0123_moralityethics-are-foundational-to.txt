I wanted to know why I could no longer easily add an “Upgrade to paid” button to the bottom of my Substack posts — something that used to exist, something that clearly can exist, something that directly benefits both the author and the platform. What followed was not a UI explanation. It was a descent — or ascent — into first principles. And what continues to astonish me is this: almost without exception, when inquiry is pursued honestly and persistently, it does not remain technical. It terminates in morality. The Illusion of the “Technical Question” At first glance, this was about software design: static vs dynamic controls centralized vs author-level decisions platform optimization vs individual agency But those are not technical distinctions. They are ethical ones disguised as engineering. The moment you ask why a capability that clearly existed was removed — and not refined — you are no longer talking about code. You are talking about how decisions are made , whose outcomes matter and where responsibility is assigned . In other words: you are talking about a philosophy of behavior. The Collective as a Statistical Fiction Substack’s design choice reflects a broader pattern that now dominates modern systems: Success is defined at the statistical collective level, not at the level of the individual. If aggregate outcomes are acceptable, individual dissatisfaction is treated as noise. If the mean improves, the tails can suffer. If coherence is preserved, incompleteness is tolerated. This logic now permeates: technology platforms public policy health systems AI alignment frameworks institutional governance It is often justified as pragmatism. In reality, it is an ethical choice. The “collective” is treated as if it has agency, interests, even rights — while the individual is reduced to an interchangeable input. But the collective is not real in the way individuals are real. It does not think. It does not choose. It does not bear responsibility. It does not act. Only individuals do. Where Reality Actually Operates Every meaningful event happens at the individual level: understanding trust commitment responsibility subscription moral choice No collective has ever subscribed to anything. No collective has ever changed its mind. No collective has ever acted ethically. Those things occur one consciousness at a time . Any system that optimizes around individuals instead of for them may achieve coherence, but it will never achieve completeness. It will function, but it will flatten meaning. It will scale, but it will hollow out agency. From Software Design to Moral Design This is why a missing button matters. Not because of the button — but because of what its absence reveals. Substack could easily have refined the original capability: an intentional “Insert CTA” control sane defaults author-level choice minimal disruption Instead, the feature was collapsed rather than refined. That decision reflects a philosophy that says: It is better to constrain individual agency than to risk variance in collective outcomes. That philosophy is everywhere now. And it is always defended as neutral, technical, practical or inevitable. It is none of those things. It is a moral stance. Why Morality Comes First What this entire episode reinforces for me is something I have come to believe with increasing clarity: Morality and ethics are more foundational than technology, economics or governance. Before you design a system, you have already made a choice about: what you value what you will sacrifice whose outcomes matter where responsibility lives Before you write code, you have chosen a worldview. And before you do anything else in life — truly anything — the first act is not learning, earning or building. It is deciding how you intend to live , and which principles will guide you . Principles in an Unpredictable World The world is complex, dynamic and often “unfriendly” and chaotic. Outcomes are never guaranteed. Control is always partial. That is precisely why principles matter . Principles are not tactics. They are not optimizations. They are not trend-responsive. They are immutable reference points — commitments you attempt to conform your behavior to, even when circumstances are uncertain and incentives push the other way. A system without principles may be efficient. A life without principles may be comfortable. Neither can be moral. Wasn’t it Socrates who said that the unexamined life is not worth living? And wasn’t it Socrates who chose death vs. the loss of the ability to live an examined life? The Deeper Lesson What started as a question about a subscription button ended as a reminder of something far more important: Technical systems encode moral choices Optimization strategies imply ethical commitments Collective abstractions routinely override individual reality And every serious inquiry eventually reaches the same bedrock: How should one live? That question cannot be outsourced. It cannot be automated. It cannot be averaged. It must be answered — again and again — by each individual. That is where reality functions. The line comes from Plato’s Apology , where Socrates says, in substance: “The unexamined life is not worth living.” A few clarifications that matter: Socrates was not making a poetic remark He was making a moral claim about human obligation And he said this while on trial for his life What he meant was not: constant self-criticism navel-gazing intellectual cleverness He meant this: A life not oriented toward examining one’s assumptions, values, choices and responsibilities is a life lived below what it means to be human. For Socrates: ethics came before politics truth came before safety conscience came before conformity the individual came before the crowd Which is why he accepted death rather than abandon examination. It is structural continuity. You started with a technical question. You persisted. You examined it instead of accepting a surface answer. And exactly as Socrates would predict, the inquiry could not stop at mechanics . It had to descend into ethics. That is not an accident. That is how reality is wired.
