Blazer I recently asked, half-jokingly, if the Earth was flat. What astonishes me isn’t the claim itself, but how many otherwise intelligent people actually believe it. And when you look closely, you uncover something far more important than the shape of the planet. You uncover the fractures in how human beings now come to know anything at all. Before we get there, consider how trivial it is to demonstrate the Earth isn’t flat. A friend of mine offered one of the most elegant proofs imaginable. He plotted a path on a map about a mile long, calculated what the distance would be on a flat Earth versus a spherical one, then walked it and measured it. The measurement matched the spherical prediction exactly. No NASA. No satellites. No experts. Just a map, a measuring wheel, some geometry and shoes. This is an analog sovereignty experiment—something any human can perform that bypasses every institutional intermediary. You don’t need authority. You don’t need credentials. You verify reality for yourself. And yet, despite endless analog proofs—ships disappearing hull-first beyond the horizon, round shadows during lunar eclipses, stars shifting with latitude, constellations that only exist in certain hemispheres—flat Earth belief is growing. The real question isn’t how to convince someone the Earth is round. It’s why so many find the flat Earth worldview comforting or compelling. The Epistemic Break Flat Earth belief arises less from lack of intelligence and more from a rupture in how people relate to knowledge. When institutions lose credibility, people often reject not only institutional claims but the entire framework in which claims are evaluated. The danger is that once the evaluative framework collapses, anything becomes plausible. In that void, alternative cosmologies flourish. The Emotional Payoff Flat Earth offers something powerful: a sense of mastery over a world that feels deceptive and chaotic. The believer can say, “I see what others cannot.” It becomes a posture of defiance. A symbolic rejection of the systems that have misled, censored, or manipulated them in other domains. Once a person steps into this stance, evidence is no longer evidence. It becomes part of the thing they are resisting. Re-Centering the Human There is also a metaphysical lure. A spherical Earth places human beings on a small planet drifting in a vast, indifferent cosmos. A flat Earth re-centers the human experience. It feels crafted, enclosed, intentional. It feels like a stage built for us. In a universe of spheres, our home is unique - and in turn we are special. In a culture that increasingly treats humans as data points, nodes or programmable matter, the desire for a universe shaped around the human perspective becomes understandable. Analog Reality Still Stands Which returns us to the simple experiments. Analog reality does not lie. It cannot be gaslit. Digital narratives can twist perception, but a measuring wheel does not. A shadow at noon does not. The visible horizon does not. When people lose contact with direct, embodied, analog experience, they become vulnerable to elaborate counter-realities. When they re-engage with direct perception—even as simply as walking a mile and measuring it—they restore an ancient bond between consciousness and the world. Flat Earth thrives where analog experience has been replaced with mediated, curated or weaponized information. The Larger Lesson Flat Earth is not the disease. It is a symptom of a deeper destabilization in how people orient themselves toward truth. It marks the point where confusion becomes identity, and defiance replaces discernment. But the path back is remarkably simple. It begins with reclaiming the birthright of direct perception. It begins with analog sovereignty. It begins with verifying reality with your own two hands. When you do that, you no longer need to fear deception. You step outside the reach of those who would deceive. Everything else is noise.
