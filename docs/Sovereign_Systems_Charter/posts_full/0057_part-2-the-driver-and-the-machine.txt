Part 2: The Driver and the Machine - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Part 2: The Driver and the Machine Performance, Judgment and Responsibility in the Age of Superior Machines harry blazer Feb 03, 2026 2 2 Share A Study in Delegation Without Abdication We began with an analogy drawn from racing: the relationship between a driver and a machine operating at the limits of performance. What started as a discussion of balance, braking, and commitment gradually revealed something deeper — a structural model for how humans must relate to increasingly capable machines. That analogy now needs refinement. Because unlike the past, we are no longer speculating about machine superiority. We are living inside it. Autonomous systems already drive faster laps than humans. Drone aircraft already outperform piloted fighters in many combat regimes. Cars routinely override driver inputs for safety. Machines dominate chess, Go and other combinatorially explosive domains. Pattern recognition systems now exceed human accuracy in medical imaging and legal discovery. This is not theoretical. It is operational reality. Any framework that insists humans must always retain control is therefore unserious. But any framework that celebrates surrender without qualification is catastrophic. So the question is no longer whether humans should relinquish control to machines. The question is: what kind of control and under what authority . The false binary: control vs surrender Public discourse often frames this issue incorrectly, as a binary choice: Either humans remain “in control,” Or machines take over. This framing is obsolete. Modern systems already operate through layered delegation. Humans no longer modulate brake pressure, manage engine timing, or calculate ballistic trajectories in real time. We delegate execution constantly — and rightly so. The error is not delegation itself. The error is confusing delegation of execution with delegation of judgment . A necessary distinction: execution domains vs judgment domains To reason clearly, we must separate two fundamentally different kinds of decision spaces. 1. Execution domains (computational excellence) These are domains characterized by: Clearly defined objectives Formalizable constraints Measurable success criteria High dimensionality Severe time pressure Examples include: Optimal racing lines and vehicle dynamics Collision avoidance and emergency braking Target tracking and interception Game move selection (chess, Go) Pattern recognition in imaging Large-scale document analysis In these domains, machines outperform humans because they are better suited to the task. Delegating execution here is not abdication — it is competence . Insisting on human supremacy in execution domains is like insisting on manual navigation when GPS is demonstrably superior. It is not principled; it is nostalgic. 2. Judgment domains (normative authority) These are domains characterized by: Value tradeoffs Moral or ethical consequence Ambiguous or contested goals Novelty that breaks prior models (more on this soon) Irreversible harm The need for accountability Examples include: Whether lethal force should be deployed What level of risk is acceptable to impose on others How justice should be interpreted or balanced What constitutes appropriate medical care for a particular patient When to override or shut down an automated system Who bears responsibility when harm occurs These domains are not computational problems. They are moral and political problems . And here, delegation is not merely dangerous — it is illegitimate. Why performance does not confer authority Machines can optimize within a frame. They cannot justify the frame itself. A system may choose the statistically optimal evasive maneuver. It cannot explain why that optimization is ethically acceptable. A model may recommend a sentence, a diagnosis or an air strike. It cannot bear guilt. It cannot be punished. It cannot be forgiven. Authority without accountability is not intelligence — it is power without standing . This is the core category error driving modern abdication. Returning to the racing analogy — now sharpened A modern race car already makes thousands of decisions per second that no human could manage: Traction control Stability modulation Brake force distribution Letting the machine do this is not surrender. But the driver still decides: Whether to attack Whether to defend Whether conditions justify risk Whether the race is worth winning The car may be faster. Only the driver decides why speed matters . That distinction scales directly to AI. Where abdication actually begins Abdication does not begin when machines outperform humans. It begins when humans: Stop understanding what is being optimized Accept outputs without interrogating underlying values Defer judgment because a system “knows better” Lose the ability — or the courage — to override Can no longer say who is responsible when harm occurs At that point, execution has quietly become authority. And sovereignty is lost without announcement. A refined principle for a machine-superior world We can now state the principle cleanly, without denial or romanticism: Humans may delegate execution to machines, but must never delegate moral authority, goal-setting or responsibility for consequences. Or more tightly: You can surrender control of how something is done, but never of whether it should be done, nor of who answers for it. This formulation survives reality as it actually exists. Why this matters now Because the systems we are discussing are not future systems. They are being deployed as we speak. The societies that thrive will not be those that resist machine superiority — nor those that worship it — but those that draw hard boundaries around judgment, responsibility and moral authority , even as they exploit machine performance to the fullest. The future is not human versus machine. It is humans who retain judgment with machines versus humans who surrender judgment to them. Both will enjoy performance. Only one remains sovereign. ADDENDUM: What do we mean by “Novelty that breaks (invalidates)prior models” as a judgment domain. It does not mean: “Something unexpected might happen” (that’s always true) “Models are imperfect” (too weak to matter) “Humans are magical at uncovering the non-possible” It means something much more precise: A model can only act correctly inside the space of situations it was designed, trained, or constrained to recognize. When reality steps outside that space, the model has no principled way to know it is wrong. That’s the danger. The key distinction: surprise vs model invalidation There are two kinds of “new” situations: 1. In-distribution novelty (safe) Variations the model was designed to handle New combinations of known variables Noise, edge cases, parameter drift Examples: A different braking coefficient on wet asphalt A slightly different tumor presentation A new legal argument using known precedents Here, machines often outperform humans. 2. Out-of-distribution novelty (dangerous) This is what the phrase refers to. Situations where: The underlying assumptions no longer hold The objective function no longer matches reality A new variable exists that the model cannot represent The meaning of “success” has changed Here, machines fail silently . They don’t panic. They don’t hesitate. They don’t ask “does this still apply?” They just optimize — confidently and incorrectly . A racing example (since this is where we started) Imagine an autonomous race system trained on: Clean track Known grip evolution Standard car behavior Now introduce novelty : Oil dropped mid-corner by another car A damaged suspension component A marshal standing in an unexpected location A tire delaminating in a way never seen in training A human driver may: Sense something “off” before identifying it Abort, lift, or choose caution without full understanding A machine will: Continue executing the optimal line Because nothing in its model says the world has changed That’s novelty invalidating the model. Why this matters more than raw error Models are not dangerous because they are wrong sometimes. They are dangerous because: They do not know when they are wrong. Humans have a meta-capacity: “This doesn’t feel right” “The assumptions might no longer apply” “I don’t know enough to proceed” That hesitation is not inefficiency. It is epistemic self-awareness . Every model assumes: A closed set of variables A stable mapping between inputs and outcomes A meaningful objective function Novelty breaks one of those. When that happens: Optimization becomes mis-optimization Speed amplifies error Confidence masks failure This is why novelty is not just “another edge case.” It is a category break . Why machines can’t fix this internally (yet) A model cannot: Invent new variables on the fly Decide its own objective is invalid Recognize a moral or contextual shift Say “stop — the frame itself may be wrong” Any “novelty detection” system is still bounded by: What it was trained to treat as novel Thresholds chosen by humans Definitions supplied in advance Which brings us back to responsibility. Why novelty forces human judgment to remain upstream Because someone must decide: What counts as “outside the model” When to slow down or halt execution When to override optimization When to accept degraded performance in exchange for safety or legitimacy That decision is not computational. It is normative and contextual . And it must be owned. The clean takeaway Here is the distilled meaning, stripped of abstraction: A machine can execute perfectly right up until the moment the world stops matching its assumptions — and it will not know when that moment arrives. Humans exist in the system precisely to recognize that moment. That’s what “novelty can invalidate the model” means.
