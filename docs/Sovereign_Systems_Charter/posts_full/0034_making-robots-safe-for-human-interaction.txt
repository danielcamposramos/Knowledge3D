Making Robots Safe for Human Interaction - by harry blazer harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in Making Robots Safe for Human Interaction Why Alignment Failed and What Must Replace It harry blazer Feb 09, 2026 1 Share The first mistake we made with artificial intelligence was believing that safety could be achieved by improving the internal character of the system . We tried to make AI: ethical, aligned, well-intentioned, value-aware, “moral.” That effort failed — not because it was naïve, but because it misunderstood the nature of power. Robotics forces us to confront that failure openly. Why “making systems moral” does not work Moral reasoning works when: the agent is bounded, the agent is embodied, the agent bears consequences, the agent is accountable, the agent can suffer loss. Machines meet none of these conditions. Even worse, capability growth overwhelms moral constraint . As systems become faster, stronger and more autonomous, any internal “values layer” becomes subordinate to performance pressure, optimization and mission scope. This was already true with language models. It becomes catastrophic with embodied systems. A robot does not need to be evil to be dangerous. It only needs to be effective . The lesson of AGI applied to robotics The turning point in AI governance came when a hard truth emerged: Any system capable of general reasoning can overrule any rule embedded within it. Robotics inherits this problem and multiplies it by force. If a robot’s safety depends on: internal policies, software-only constraints, cloud-based oversight, “don’t do harm” directives, then safety exists only until the first failure, exploit, update, emergency or adversarial condition. Safety cannot live inside the agent. It must live around the agent. The correct framing: tolerance, not trust We must abandon the language of trust. Robots should not be trusted. They should be tolerated under strict conditions . This is how we handle: heavy machinery, hazardous materials, explosives, nuclear systems, aircraft, pharmaceuticals. Not by assuming goodwill — but by imposing constraints that cannot be bypassed by intent or intelligence . Robotics must be treated the same way. The Illuminator’s proper role (redefined) The Illuminator is not a conscience. It is not a moral tutor. It is not an alignment engine. It is a sovereignty boundary system . Its function is fourfold: I. Classification Determine what a system is , by capability, not by marketing: embodied or not, mobile or fixed, force-capable or non-coercive, dual-use transformable or not. If it can become coercive, it is treated as coercive. II. Constraint verification Verify — not assume — that hard boundaries exist: separation between decision and actuation, upstream interlocks, safe-state enforcement, update gating for authority changes, tamper-evident audit trails. No proof, no permission to operate. III. Monitoring and alarming The Illuminator’s original function still matters: detect boundary violations, flag drift, surface unsafe patterns, preserve forensic truth. But monitoring alone is insufficient without enforceable constraints. IV. Triggering consequences When violations occur, consequences must be automatic: liability activation, insurance invalidation, procurement disqualification, injunction eligibility. No discretion. No quiet waivers. Why bureaucracy must not be the center A centralized AI or robotics authority will fail for the same reason others have failed: capture, secrecy, emergency exemptions, mission creep. So the Illuminator must function as: a public standard , not an agency, a verification grammar , not a command hierarchy, a trigger system , not a discretionary enforcer. Think constitution + audit protocol + liability switch. Civil space as a protected domain A critical boundary must be drawn and defended: Civil space is not a testing ground for autonomous coercion. In civil space: robots may assist, robots may inform, robots may transport, robots may clean, robots may navigate. But they may not: pursue, restrain, compel, enforce, punish, decide compliance. Any system capable of those functions belongs in a different category — one subject to extraordinary restrictions or outright prohibition. All domestic coercive authority must remain under direct, contemporaneous human control, with personal accountability and constitutional liability attached to the human actor. Human law enforcement operates within a framework of accountability, however imperfect. The remedy for unlawful human force is legal redress, not automation of coercion. The existence of injustice does not justify replacing accountable human authority with unaccountable machines. The Modern Day Posse Comitatus Provision No autonomous or semi-autonomous robotic system capable of force, restraint, pursuit, or compelled compliance may be deployed by the government in domestic civil space, nor used against the civilian population under any circumstances. Emergency conditions do not create new authority for autonomous coercion; they only test whether constitutional limits are real. Safety begins with refusal, not compliance This is the conceptual inversion most people miss. Human safety does not begin with systems behaving correctly. It begins with humans retaining the right and capacity to refuse . A robot is “safe” only if: its presence is consensual, its function is bounded, its authority is subordinate, its failure modes are survivable, its removal is possible. If a system cannot be refused, exited or neutralized, it is not safe — regardless of certification. The order we must not violate again The correct order is now clear: Establish sovereign self-defense Prohibit non-consensual autonomous coercion Impose non-bypassable constraints Verify compliance continuously Allow only what survives all of the above We reversed this order with AI. We will not survive reversing it again with robotics. The real question of the embodied age The question is not: “Can we make robots safe?” The real question is: “Can we integrate machines into human space without dissolving human sovereignty?” If the answer is no, then the technology must yield — not the human. Closing statement Safety is not a feeling, a policy, or an aspiration. It is a condition enforced by boundaries that power cannot overrule. The Illuminator exists to make those boundaries visible, verifiable and non-negotiable. Only then can robots be tolerated in a world meant for humans. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
