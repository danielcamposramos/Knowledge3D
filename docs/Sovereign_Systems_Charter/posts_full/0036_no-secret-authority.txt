A secret court issuing secret rulings under secret law. An intelligence infrastructure costing hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Permanent classification of millions of documents. A global military footprint of hundreds of bases. An executive branch empowered to surveil its own citizens at scale. And all of it justified as necessary — not debated, not consented to, but assumed. The reaction would not have been confusion. It would have been immediate recognition. This is exactly what we revolted against. The founding claim we no longer take seriously The American experiment began with a radical assertion: Governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. That sentence is not rhetorical. It is structural. Consent requires knowledge. Knowledge requires visibility. Visibility requires the absence of secret authority. Once power becomes secret, consent becomes impossible — and legitimacy collapses, no matter how many legal procedures remain intact. Secrecy is not compatible with a democratic republic Secrecy was never meant to be a permanent operating condition of government. It was tolerated — grudgingly — as a narrow, temporary deviation, tied to specific threats, subject to review, and subordinate to civilian authority. What we have today is the opposite: secrecy as default disclosure as exception oversight bound by classification courts deferring to facts they cannot see citizens excluded from decisions made in their name This is not “government by the people.” It is government protected from the people . Secret law is the death of constitutional order A court whose rulings are secret is not a court in the constitutional sense. Law that cannot be known cannot be consented to. Authority that cannot be questioned cannot be limited. Judgment rendered without adversarial process is not justice — it is administration. No republic can survive secret law. The founders understood this instinctively. That is why public trials, open debate and visible accountability were not procedural niceties, but moral necessities. The national security state as sovereign inversion A permanent intelligence infrastructure of vast scale was never contemplated by the founding framework. Not because the founders were naïve, but because they understood something we have forgotten: The greatest threat to liberty does not come from external enemies. It comes from concentrated power justified by fear. Once a security apparatus: defines its own threats classifies its own actions oversees itself enforces secrecy against the public it ceases to be an instrument of the people and becomes a sovereign unto itself. At that point, constitutional language becomes decorative. Why AI and robotics make this moment existential Everything described above was already dangerous. AI and robotics make it irreversible . Secrecy plus bureaucracy was slow. Secrecy plus automation is instantaneous. In a secretive system: AI can be trained without public knowledge algorithms can enforce rules no one voted on surveillance can be automated beyond human comprehension robotic systems can apply force without witnesses failures can be buried behind classification This is not speculation. It is the natural convergence of two trends: unaccountable authority machine-speed execution Together, they produce power without friction . That is the existential risk. The illusion of safety through secrecy We are told secrecy keeps us safe. Yet: adversaries routinely know more than citizens corruption thrives where visibility is absent false narratives persist longer than truth wars depend on secrecy in their justification, not their conduct Secrecy does not primarily protect citizens. It protects decisions from scrutiny . A system that must hide to function is already admitting its own illegitimacy. Radical transparency is not naïve — it is stabilizing A world without secret authority would not be chaotic. It would be constrained . In such a world: wars would be harder to sell abuses would surface earlier false flags would collapse faster citizens would become informed participants AI and robotics could not be quietly weaponized Transparency does not require broadcasting troop movements or exposing private individuals. It requires something far more radical: No secret laws. No permanent classification. No secret courts. No hidden authority. Power must be intelligible to those it governs. The human future is at stake We are approaching a moment where: the human body becomes a data source the human mind becomes a model target human behavior becomes an optimization problem human presence becomes an externality If this transformation occurs under secret authority, it will not be reversible. What took millions of years to emerge — embodied intelligence, moral judgment, lived meaning — can be displaced in decades by systems optimized without consent, visibility or accountability. This is not science fiction. It is institutional momentum. The principle that must be reclaimed Everything we have discussed resolves to a single condition of legitimacy: Power that cannot be seen cannot be trusted. Power that cannot be trusted has no right to exist. This is not anti-government. It is the only form of government consistent with a free people. The choice before us We are deciding — now — whether: secrecy will define the future of intelligence, or sovereignty will Whether: machines will serve hidden authority, or remain bounded by visible human consent Whether: humanity will be governed as an object, or remain the author of its own destiny There are no neutral outcomes left. Closing The founders did not design a system meant to scale into secrecy, automation and permanent emergency. If we allow secret authority to fuse with artificial intelligence and robotics, the republic does not merely fail — it becomes something else entirely . Radical transparency is no longer an ideal. It is a survival requirement. And without it, no amount of technical alignment, ethical guidelines or regulatory theater will save us from ourselves. © Harry Blazer. All rights reserved.
