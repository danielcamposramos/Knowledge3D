The Second Amendment in the Age of Robotics harry’s Substack Subscribe Sign in The Second Amendment in the Age of Robotics A Sovereign Self-Defense Doctrine for the Embodied Age harry blazer Feb 08, 2026 1 Share Civilization rests on a simple moral truth: no person may rightfully destroy another person’s sovereignty. Sovereignty means more than abstract liberty. It means the lived reality that a human being can refuse coercion, resist force and defend life, loved ones and community when threatened. When that capacity disappears, rights become theoretical and law becomes theater. The rise of embodied robotics forces us to confront this truth again. Why robotics changes everything For most of modern history, the primary threat to individual sovereignty came from other humans. The law of self-defense evolved accordingly. The Second Amendment did not exist to glorify violence; it existed to preserve parity of agency — the idea that no individual should be rendered helpless in the face of overwhelming force. Robotics changes the terrain. A machine with limbs, actuators and autonomy introduces a new class of force into human space: tireless, faster than human reflex, stronger than human bone, immune to fear, pain and hesitation. This is not speculation. Industrial robots have already killed people through malfunction, error and poor safeguards. What is now being proposed goes far beyond fenced factory arms: mobile, adaptive systems operating in public and private spaces. Once embodied, any autonomous mechanical system is a latent coercive actor , regardless of intent. Intent does not matter when force becomes asymmetric. Sovereignty cannot survive asymmetry A foundational principle must be stated plainly: A being cannot be sovereign if it is physically incapable of resisting coercion. When bone and skin are placed against machines of superior speed and strength, and the human has no local means of defense, sovereignty collapses in fact — no matter what the law claims on paper. This is why the right of self-defense is a natural right. It does not arise from statute. It arises from the moral structure of reality. The famous aphorism captures this truth succinctly: “God created man. Equality required tools.” (A euphemized version of “God created Man. Winchester Made Them Equal”) The point is not violence. The point is parity . 3. The mistake we must not repeat With artificial intelligence, society attempted to solve the sovereignty problem by making systems “ethical,” “aligned,” or “safe.” That effort failed for a simple reason: capability outruns intention . No system can be trusted solely because it claims to be benign. No safeguard survives every failure mode. No bureaucracy remains incorruptible. No emergency power remains temporary. Robotics magnifies this failure because it closes the loop between decision and physical force. Therefore, we must not make the same mistake again. Self-defense must precede system safety. The Sovereign Self-Defense Doctrine In the age of embodied autonomy, the right of self-defense must be reaffirmed and extended in principle, even as technologies change. The doctrine rests on four pillars: I. Sovereignty is local The right of self-defense belongs to the individual, not to centralized authority. It must not depend on networks, permissions, vendors or emergency goodwill. A right that requires permission is not a right. II. Coercion triggers defense Whenever a human faces non-consensual autonomous force, the right of self-defense activates — because no autonomous system can possess lawful authority, moral agency, or accountability. This includes: physical threat, restraint, pursuit, forced compliance, denial of exit. The source of the threat is irrelevant. The effect is decisive. III. Parity must be preserved If society permits systems with superhuman physical capability to operate in civil space, then individuals must retain lawful means sufficient to restore parity when faced with non-consensual force. Without parity, consent is meaningless. IV. Self-defense is last resort, not first principle This doctrine does not glorify confrontation. It recognizes that defense exists because systems fail , safeguards break and power corrupts. A civilization that forbids last-resort self-defense guarantees first-resort tyranny. What this doctrine does not mean Clarity matters. This doctrine does not : call for militarization of civilian life, endorse aggression, specify weapons or tactics, invite vigilantism, override proportionality or accountability. It simply asserts that no person may be rendered defenseless by design . Why regulation alone is insufficient Some argue that robots can be made safe through regulation, oversight and design standards. These are necessary — but never sufficient. History teaches us: regulations are waived in crises, agencies are captured over time, safeguards are bypassed under pressure, laws protect those who write them. Self-defense exists precisely because governance is fallible. A system that forbids self-defense while promising perfect safety is asking citizens to gamble their sovereignty on institutional virtue. That is not realism; it is abdication. The order matters Only after sovereignty is secured does safety become meaningful. First: affirm the right of local refusal, preserve individual defensive parity, prohibit non-consensual autonomous coercion. Then: design robots for safe human interaction, impose architectural constraints, build monitoring, auditing and governance systems. This was the mistake with AI: attempting alignment before establishing inviolable human authority. We will not repeat it. A civilization’s test Every era faces a test that reveals whether rights are real or rhetorical. In the embodied age, the test is this: Will humans remain sovereign in their own physical world, or will they be asked to trust systems they cannot resist? The answer cannot be deferred. It cannot be centralized. It cannot be postponed until after deployment. Self-defense is not an obstacle to progress. It is the condition under which progress remains human. Closing statement Where autonomous force exists, the right of individual self-defense must evolve to preserve parity — or sovereignty ceases to exist in fact. That principle guided the Second Amendment in an earlier age. It must guide us again.
